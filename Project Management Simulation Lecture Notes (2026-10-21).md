# Project Management Simulation Lecture Notes

## Lecture Focus

This lecture examines how project managers should develop a strategy before beginning a project simulation.

The central lesson is that a project manager should not immediately begin changing settings through trial and error. Instead, the manager should first understand the project environment, identify likely risks, form an initial strategy, apply that strategy, monitor the results, and reflect on the final outcome.

The lecture discusses two main scenarios:

1. Scenario B, which involves employee losses, competition for employees, schedule pressure, meeting choices, training decisions, and budget tradeoffs.

2. Scenario F, which involves uncertain technology, artificial intelligence features, a competing company, possible scope changes, resource competition, and management disagreement.

# 1. The Project Management Learning Cycle

An effective project management process can be organized into five stages.

## 1.1 Understand the situation

Before making decisions, collect as much information as possible about:

1. The required scope

2. The target completion date

3. The available budget

4. The required product quality

5. The required employee skill level

6. The number of available employees

7. Possible staff losses

8. Technology uncertainty

9. Competitor activity

10. Management expectations

11. Possible future changes

12. Available outsourcing options

A project manager should treat every relevant piece of information seriously. Even an informal comment from an employee may reveal an important risk.

## 1.2 Anticipate what may happen

The project manager should identify:

1. What may go well

2. What may go wrong

3. Which risks are likely

4. Which risks would have the greatest effect

5. Which decisions would be difficult to reverse

This process creates an initial understanding of the project environment.

## 1.3 Form a strategy

A strategy is a working hypothesis about how the project should be managed.

A useful strategy answers questions such as:

1. How large should the team be?

2. What skill level should the team have?

3. How much training should occur early?

4. How many prototypes should be created?

5. How aggressive should the schedule be?

6. Is it acceptable to exceed the budget?

7. When should outsourcing be used?

8. How should the manager respond if employees leave?

9. How should meetings change as the project progresses?

10. What should happen if the scope increases?

## 1.4 Apply and adjust the strategy

The initial strategy should guide the project, but it should not prevent reasonable adjustments.

A manager may need to change:

1. Team size

2. Training frequency

3. Meeting frequency

4. Target completion date

5. Outsourcing level

6. Prototype frequency

7. Budget use

8. Work intensity

Changes should be purposeful responses to project information, not random experimentation.

## 1.5 Reflect on the outcome

At the end of the project, ask:

1. What worked well?

2. What did not work well?

3. Which assumptions were correct?

4. Which assumptions were incorrect?

5. Which risks occurred?

6. Which risks were missed?

7. How many attempts were required?

8. Could the same result have been reached more efficiently?

9. What should be repeated in the next project?

10. What should be changed in the next project?

Reflection converts experience into project management knowledge.

# 2. Why Trial and Error Is Not Enough

A simulation may allow many attempts, but an actual project usually does not.

Some students completed the simulation after approximately six attempts. Others needed approximately ten to seventeen attempts.

Repeated attempts can be useful for learning, but an actual organization usually cannot restart a project sixteen times. Each failed attempt would consume time, money, employee effort, and organizational trust.

The goal is therefore not only to achieve a high score. The goal is to achieve a strong result through informed planning and efficient execution.

A good project manager should reduce unnecessary experimentation by:

1. Studying the project description

2. Learning from previous scenarios

3. Asking other people about their experiences

4. Developing an independent strategy

5. Testing a clear hypothesis

6. Recording the effect of each decision

# 3. Learning From Other People

Receiving advice from classmates or colleagues is not automatically unethical.

In real projects, organizations regularly learn from:

1. Previous projects

2. Experienced employees

3. Industry practices

4. Consultants

5. Competitors

6. Research

7. Lessons learned databases

8. Informal conversations

Learning from another person can save time and prevent repeated mistakes.

However, borrowed advice should not replace independent thinking.

The project manager remains responsible for:

1. Understanding the project

2. Evaluating whether the advice applies

3. Developing a personal strategy

4. Adjusting the strategy to fit the situation

5. Explaining the reason behind each decision

The best approach combines shared experience with independent analysis.

# 4. Scenario B

## 4.1 Main characteristics

Scenario B appears to extend an earlier scenario, but with more demanding requirements.

Important characteristics include:

1. A higher required skill level

2. A demanding completion target

3. Possible employee losses

4. A rival organization hiring employees

5. Temporary difficulty replacing lost employees

6. The need to balance budget and schedule performance

7. The need to train and coordinate the team

8. The need to decide whether to rehire employees later

This scenario demonstrates that a strategy that worked in an easier project may need to be modified.

# 5. Scenario B Strategies Discussed in Class

## 5.1 Invest in training early

Several students used one on one meetings during the first few weeks.

The purpose was to:

1. Increase employee skill

2. Improve productivity later

3. Prevent complaints about insufficient training

4. Use the early period when employees were less stressed

5. Prepare employees for later schedule pressure

One student used approximately three one on one sessions during the first few weeks, reduced this to approximately two sessions, and later removed most training.

The reasoning was that training is most valuable before schedule pressure becomes severe.

## 5.2 Change the meeting mix over time

Different meeting types serve different purposes.

### One on one meetings

Useful for:

1. Training

2. Individual feedback

3. Skill development

4. Understanding personal concerns

These meetings were most useful early in the project.

### Stand up meetings

Useful for:

1. Short coordination updates

2. Identifying immediate problems

3. Maintaining team awareness

4. Responding to rapid changes

Stand ups became more important as development activity increased.

### Status review meetings

Useful for:

1. Reviewing overall progress

2. Comparing actual performance with the plan

3. Identifying schedule concerns

4. Supporting management decisions

Some students alternated between approximately three and four stand ups or status reviews depending on team stress and project needs.

## 5.3 Avoid keeping every meeting at the same level

Meetings should respond to the project stage.

A possible meeting strategy is:

1. Begin with more training and one on one meetings

2. Gradually reduce training

3. Increase coordination meetings during intense production

4. Reduce unnecessary meetings near completion

5. Avoid adding meetings only because employees complain

A meeting has a cost because it uses time that could otherwise be spent on project work.

## 5.4 Start with a larger team

Several strong results came from starting with approximately seven or eight employees.

Examples discussed in class included:

1. Starting with eight employees

2. Falling to five employees when the rival hired team members

3. Returning to eight employees when hiring became possible again

Another example involved:

1. Starting with seven employees

2. Falling to four employees

3. Returning to seven employees later

The reasoning was that a larger starting team provides protection against employee losses.

If a project begins with only three employees and loses two, only one remains. This can severely damage productivity.

## 5.5 Rehire quickly when hiring becomes possible

Some students immediately replaced lost employees once the restriction ended.

This strategy restored production capacity and supported early completion.

However, rehiring also increases cost. The decision should therefore consider whether the productivity gained is worth the additional expense.

## 5.6 Use aggressive completion targets

One successful approach was to target completion much earlier than the official deadline.

For example:

1. The official target might be around week fifteen

2. The student might aim for week eight or week ten

3. The student might temporarily relax the target during a stressful period

4. The student might restore the aggressive target after rehiring

An aggressive target can create pressure, so it must be managed carefully.

## 5.7 Temporarily relax the target during a crisis

When several employees left, one student extended the target date from approximately week eight to week ten.

After replacing the employees, the student returned the target to week eight.

This shows that a target can be adjusted temporarily in response to a major disruption.

The important point is that the student did not abandon the overall strategy. The student changed the target for a specific reason and restored it when conditions improved.

## 5.8 Accept budget overruns when the scoring structure rewards speed

One example involved:

1. A target budget of approximately 39,000 dollars

2. Actual spending of approximately 56,000 dollars

3. Completion around week eight

4. A final score above 800

The project exceeded its budget, but early completion compensated for the extra cost.

This does not mean that budget discipline is unimportant. It means that project objectives must be prioritized according to their value.

If early delivery creates significantly more value than budget savings, spending more may be rational.

## 5.9 Understand the budget and schedule tradeoff

Reducing team size lowers labor cost, but it also reduces productivity.

If too many employees are removed:

1. Work slows down

2. Team knowledge is lost

3. Skill capacity decreases

4. The project may finish later

5. Schedule penalties may exceed budget savings

The manager must compare the value of:

1. Staying under budget

2. Finishing early

3. Completing the required scope

4. Maintaining quality

Some students found that early completion produced a much larger score improvement than staying significantly under budget.

## 5.10 Reduce staff gradually

When approaching completion, it may be reasonable to reduce team size to control cost.

However, the reduction should be gradual and connected to actual productivity.

A sudden large reduction may remove important skills and cause the project to slow down.

A better method is:

1. Observe remaining work

2. Observe current productivity

3. Identify which skills are still needed

4. Remove unnecessary capacity gradually

5. Monitor the effect after each reduction

## 5.11 Use outsourcing carefully

One student kept a smaller internal team after employee losses and increased outsourcing.

This helped complete remaining work without rebuilding the entire internal team.

However, excessive outsourcing created problems:

1. The internal team became confused

2. Coordination became more difficult

3. Additional issues appeared

4. Employees sometimes felt disconnected from the work

Outsourcing is therefore useful as targeted support, not necessarily as a complete replacement for the internal team.

## 5.12 Use prototypes to maintain direction

A student reported that completing at least one prototype before team members left helped the remaining employees.

The prototype provided:

1. A concrete example

2. A clearer understanding of the product

3. A reference point for later development

4. Reduced confusion

5. Better continuity after staff losses

Without a prototype, the smaller team had less guidance and productivity slowed.

# 6. Scenario B Lessons

The strongest lessons from Scenario B are:

1. Train employees early.

2. Use one on one meetings before pressure becomes severe.

3. Change the meeting mix as the project changes.

4. Begin with enough employees to survive expected staff losses.

5. Rehire quickly when schedule performance is more valuable than budget savings.

6. Accept calculated budget overruns when early delivery creates greater value.

7. Avoid cutting the team too quickly.

8. Use outsourcing selectively.

9. Use prototypes to preserve direction and knowledge.

10. Create a strategy before beginning the simulation.

## 7.1 Main characteristics

Scenario F contains greater uncertainty and is more similar to a difficult real project.

The project involves:

1. A new building system

2. Advanced artificial intelligence and learning features

3. Technology that is still immature

4. Continuing research and development work

5. A possible competing product

6. Incomplete information about the competitor

7. A tight schedule

10. Disagreement among senior managers

11. Other projects competing for resources

13. A risk that management may change its decisions

This is not a stable project where the manager can configure everything once and simply observe the result.

# 8. Proactive and Reactive Management

## 8.1 Reactive management

Reactive management means waiting for a problem to happen and then responding.

Examples include:

1. Waiting for the technology to fail before testing it

2. Waiting for employees to leave before planning replacements

3. Waiting for scope growth before considering additional work

4. Waiting for management to remove resources before accelerating progress

Reactive management is sometimes necessary, but it is usually more expensive.

## 8.2 Proactive management

Proactive management means anticipating likely problems and preparing in advance.

Examples include:

1. Building prototypes early

2. Starting with a larger team

3. Training employees before pressure increases

4. Pursuing early completion before scope changes occur

5. Using available resources before they are redirected

6. Preparing for competitor action

7. Planning outsourcing options

An experienced project manager should be proactive whenever possible.

# 9. Risk Analysis

A risk can be evaluated through two main dimensions.

## 9.1 Probability

Probability asks:

How likely is the risk to occur?

Possible categories include:

1. Low probability

2. Medium probability

3. High probability

## 9.2 Impact

Impact asks:

How serious would the consequences be if the risk occurred?

Possible categories include:

1. Low impact

2. Medium impact

3. High impact

## 9.3 Risk priority

A risk deserves greater attention when both probability and impact are high.

A simple conceptual rule is:

Risk priority equals probability multiplied by impact.

A low probability risk may still require planning if its impact would be extremely serious.

# 10. Pandemic Example

The lecture used a possible future pandemic as an example of risk assessment.

For a project lasting approximately two years, another pandemic may be possible, but its probability might be judged as relatively low based on historical experience.

The project manager should still ask:

1. What is the probability?

2. What would the impact be?

3. Is the project prepared for remote work?

4. Are communication systems available?

5. Are suppliers vulnerable?

6. Would the schedule survive a disruption?

The point is not to predict the future perfectly. The point is to consider realistic uncertainty before it becomes a crisis.

# 11. Technology Risk in Scenario F

The new artificial intelligence technology is risky because:

1. It is still immature

2. Research and development work is continuing

3. Requirements may not be stable

4. Technical problems may appear late

5. Employees may not fully understand the technology

6. The project scope may expand after new discoveries

7. Rework may be required

Technology risk can directly affect:

1. Schedule

2. Scope

3. Budget

4. Quality

5. Employee workload

6. Required skills

# 12. Competitor Risk in Scenario F

The competitor may be working on a similar product, but the available information is incomplete and potentially unreliable.

Possible effects include:

1. Senior management may shorten the deadline

2. Management may demand additional features

3. The organization may increase spending

4. The organization may redirect employees

5. The project may be forced to release before it is fully ready

6. Employees may leave for the competitor

7. Management may cancel or redesign the project

8. Market value may decline if the competitor releases first

The professor treated competitor risk as especially serious because it is largely outside the project manager's control.

# 13. Comparing Technology Risk and Competitor Risk

Students disagreed about which risk was greater.

## 13.1 Argument that technology risk is greater

Technology risk may appear greater because:

1. The technology is known to be immature

2. Research and development work is still occurring

3. Technical uncertainty directly affects the project

4. The competitor information may be inaccurate

5. Technology issues may continue even if the competitor disappears

This position emphasizes the certainty and directness of the technical problem.

## 13.2 Argument that competitor risk is greater

Competitor risk may appear greater because:

1. It is outside the project manager's control

2. It can suddenly change management decisions

3. It can shorten the schedule dramatically

4. It can increase scope

5. It can affect budgets and staffing

6. Employees may leave for the competitor

7. The organization may demand immediate delivery

This position emphasizes impact and lack of control.

## 13.3 Professor's interpretation

The professor considered competitor risk particularly dangerous.

The reasoning was:

1. Technology problems can often be addressed through research, hiring, outsourcing, or alternative solutions.

2. Competitor actions cannot be controlled by the project manager.

3. Senior managers often react strongly to competitor activity.

4. Even uncertain competitor information may require serious attention.

5. A competitor can trigger abrupt changes in schedule, scope, resources, and strategic direction.

The broader lesson is that uncertainty does not always reduce risk. Sometimes uncertainty increases the need for preparation.

# 14. Management Disagreement as a Risk

Scenario F states that senior managers disagree about whether the organization should make such a large investment in the new technology.

This creates several risks:

1. Funding may be reduced

2. Employees may be transferred

3. Priorities may change

4. The project may be delayed

5. The project may be cancelled

6. Scope may be changed

7. Managers may demand faster results

8. Other projects may compete for the same resources

A project manager should treat disagreement among decision makers as a serious warning.

Senior managers control the continuation, funding, and strategic direction of the project.

# 15. Recommended Strategy for Scenario F

## 15.1 Build prototypes early

When technology is uncertain, prototypes provide early information.

Benefits include:

1. Revealing technical problems

2. Testing assumptions

3. Clarifying requirements

4. Improving employee understanding

5. Reducing later rework

6. Supporting management decisions

7. Showing visible progress

8. Providing evidence that the technology can work

Students found that the first prototype often revealed major technology issues.

A useful approach is to schedule the first prototype in the first week or as early as possible.

## 15.2 Use multiple prototypes

Some students used approximately four prototypes. Others tried five.

Multiple prototypes can help because each prototype tests a different stage of development.

However, prototypes consume time and resources. They should be designed to answer important questions.

## 15.3 Push hard at the beginning

The class developed a strategy of using significant resources early.

This can involve:

1. Starting with a large team

2. Hiring employees with medium high skills

3. Using extensive outsourcing

4. Conducting early training

5. Creating prototypes immediately

6. Setting an aggressive target date

7. Spending more during the first few weeks

The purpose is to achieve progress before:

1. The competitor releases its product

2. Management changes its mind

3. Resources are redirected

4. Scope expands

5. Employee availability changes

## 15.4 Produce an early visible success

An early prototype or major milestone strengthens the project manager's position.

Visible progress can:

1. Increase management confidence

2. Protect the project's resources

3. Demonstrate feasibility

4. Reduce disagreement

5. Provide information for future decisions

6. Show that the project is moving faster than expected

## 15.5 Aim for early completion

One student used:

1. Eight employees

2. Medium high skill levels

3. Four prototypes

4. An aggressive completion target

5. A reduction from eight employees to three employees only in the final week

The project finished around week nine, approximately six weeks before the original week fifteen deadline.

This strategy closely matched the proposed approach of pushing hard early.

## 15.6 Finish before major scope growth

Some students reported that additional scope appeared around week ten or week eleven.

Students who completed the project earlier avoided some of this additional work.

This suggests an important strategic lesson:

Early completion can reduce exposure to future uncertainty.

The longer a project remains open, the more opportunities there are for:

1. Scope growth

2. Competitor action

3. Management changes

4. Resource loss

5. Technical problems

6. New requirements

## 15.7 Use resources while they are available

If resources may be removed later, it may be reasonable to use them early.

For example:

1. Hire more employees early

2. Train them early

3. Complete prototypes early

4. Outsource important tasks early

5. Achieve major milestones before resources are redirected

This strategy must still be controlled. Spending early without a clear purpose is not effective.

# 16. Important Tradeoffs

## 16.1 Budget versus schedule

Spending less may delay completion.

Spending more may create earlier benefits.

The best choice depends on the value of time.

## 16.2 Team size versus coordination

A larger team provides more production capacity, but also requires more coordination.

Adding people does not guarantee proportional productivity.

## 16.3 Meetings versus productive work

Meetings can improve communication, training, and coordination.

Too many meetings reduce time available for development.

## 16.4 Training versus immediate output

Training reduces short term output but may increase later productivity.

Training is often most valuable early.

## 16.5 Internal staff versus outsourcing

Internal staff provide continuity and organizational knowledge.

Outsourcing can create flexibility and speed but may increase coordination problems.

## 16.6 Early completion versus employee stress

An aggressive target may produce a strong score and reduce exposure to later risks.

However, excessive pressure may increase stress and reduce employee satisfaction.

## 16.7 Prototype work versus final production

Prototypes consume resources but reveal uncertainty.

Skipping prototypes may appear faster initially, but can produce severe rework later.

# 17. Common Mistakes

## 17.1 Starting without a strategy

Randomly changing settings makes it difficult to learn from each attempt.

## 17.2 Copying an earlier scenario

A strategy that worked in Scenario A may fail in Scenario B or Scenario F because the environment has changed.

## 17.3 Focusing only on budget

Strict budget control may lead to late completion and a lower overall result.

## 17.4 Cutting too many employees

A rapid reduction in team size can remove important skills and slow the project.

## 17.5 Ignoring prototypes

Without prototypes, technical problems may remain hidden until they are expensive to fix.

## 17.6 Treating uncertain competitor information as irrelevant

Even incomplete information may signal a major external risk.

## 17.7 Waiting for employees to request meetings

Employees may not always identify the coordination structure they need.

The manager must use judgement.

## 17.8 Depending completely on borrowed advice

Advice is useful, but the manager must still understand the project and develop an independent plan.

## 17.9 Using outsourcing excessively

Too much outsourcing may create confusion, loss of ownership, and coordination problems.

## 17.10 Repeating the simulation without reflection

Many attempts do not automatically create learning.

Each attempt should test a specific idea.

# 18. A Practical Planning Framework

Before beginning a future scenario, complete the following process.

## Step 1: Read the entire scenario

Identify every statement about:

1. Scope

2. Schedule

3. Budget

4. Technology

5. Employees

6. Competitors

7. Management

8. Customers

9. Resources

10. Possible changes

## Step 2: Identify risks

List each risk and estimate:

1. Probability

2. Impact

3. Warning signs

4. Possible responses

## Step 3: Establish priorities

Decide which objectives matter most:

1. Early completion

2. Budget control

3. Maximum scope

4. High quality

5. Employee wellbeing

6. Learning

7. Market advantage

## Step 4: Create the initial configuration

Decide:

1. Team size

2. Skill level

3. Training level

4. Meeting frequency

5. Prototype schedule

6. Outsourcing level

7. Target completion date

8. Initial spending level

## Step 5: Define decision rules

Examples include:

1. If employees leave, maintain the core team and use limited outsourcing.

2. If hiring becomes available, rehire quickly when schedule performance is the priority.

3. If stress becomes too high, temporarily relax the target date.

4. If technology issues appear, increase prototype activity and technical support.

5. If major work is nearly complete, reduce the team gradually.

6. If management confidence declines, demonstrate progress through a visible milestone.

## Step 6: Run the project

Apply the strategy consistently while monitoring:

1. Productivity

2. Employee stress

3. Skill development

4. Budget use

5. Schedule performance

6. Remaining work

7. Scope changes

8. Technical issues

## Step 7: Record results

After each attempt, record:

1. Initial settings

2. Changes made

3. Timing of each change

4. Final score

5. Completion week

6. Final cost

7. Final scope

8. Final team size

9. Main problems

10. Main successes

## Step 8: Change only what is justified

Avoid changing many variables at once.

If too many variables change, it becomes difficult to identify which decision caused the result.

## Step 9: Reflect

State clearly:

1. What was expected

2. What actually happened

3. Why the difference occurred

4. What should be changed next time

# 19. Suggested Scenario B Plan

A reasonable starting plan for Scenario B is:

1. Begin with seven or eight employees.

2. Use medium high skill levels if affordable.

3. Provide intensive one on one training during the first few weeks.

4. Complete at least one useful prototype early.

5. Set an aggressive completion target.

8. Rehire when possible if early completion is valuable.

9. Increase stand ups and status reviews as coordination needs rise.

3. Create the first prototype immediately.

4. Plan several additional prototypes.

5. Begin with a large and capable team.

6. Use medium high skills.

7. Use outsourcing strategically.

8. Spend more early if resources may disappear later.

9. Set an aggressive target date.

10. Produce visible progress quickly.

# 21. Main Lecture Conclusions

The most important lessons are:

1. Do not begin a project without understanding the situation.

2. Develop a strategy before changing project settings.

3. Treat the strategy as a hypothesis that can be tested.

4. Learn from other people, but maintain independent judgement.

5. Be proactive rather than waiting for problems.

6. Evaluate risks through probability and impact.

7. Pay close attention to external risks that cannot be controlled.

8. Use early prototypes when technology is uncertain.

9. Use training early when later productivity depends on employee skill.

10. Match meetings to the current needs of the project.

11. Avoid reducing the team too quickly.

12. Use outsourcing carefully.

13. Recognize that early completion may be more valuable than strict budget control.

14. Push hard early when scope, resources, and management support may change.

15. Record the reasoning and result of every attempt.

16. Reflect after completion so that experience becomes reusable knowledge.

# 23. Final Summary

Successful project management requires planning before action.

A manager should collect information, identify risks, prioritize objectives, create a strategy, apply it, monitor results, adjust carefully, and reflect afterward.

Scenario B demonstrates the importance of early training, sufficient team size, controlled rehiring, meeting adjustments, and intelligent tradeoffs between cost and schedule.

Scenario F demonstrates the importance of prototypes, early progress, competitor awareness, management support, resource protection, and proactive risk management.

The simulation is not only about achieving a high score. It is practice in thinking like a project manager.
