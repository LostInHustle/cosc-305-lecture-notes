# Project Risk Management

## 1. What Is Project Risk?

Every project involves uncertainty. Project risk is an uncertain event or condition that could affect one or more project objectives if it occurs.

These objectives may include:

1. Scope
2. Schedule
3. Cost
4. Quality
5. Safety
6. Resources
7. Customer satisfaction
8. Business value

A risk is something that might happen in the future. If it has already happened, it is no longer a risk. It is now an issue that must be managed.

Risks can have positive or negative effects.

A negative risk is called a threat. For example, a key developer might become unavailable before an important deadline.

A positive risk is called an opportunity. For example, a tax reduction might lower the cost of a project.

Project teams often focus on threats because they can delay work, increase costs, reduce quality, or cause injuries. However, good risk management also considers opportunities that could benefit the project.

## 2. Sources of Project Risk

Risks can come from internal or external sources.

### Internal risks

Internal risks originate within the project or organization.

Examples include:

1. A team member becoming unavailable
2. A technology failing
3. A software licence expiring
4. Poor communication
5. Inaccurate estimates
6. Equipment failure
7. Insufficient training
8. A shortage of employees
9. Errors in the project schedule
10. Changes to project requirements

The project team usually has more control over internal risks.

### External risks

External risks originate outside the project or organization.

Examples include:

1. A competitor releasing a similar product
2. Changes in taxes
3. New government regulations
4. Supplier delays
5. Severe weather
6. Economic changes
7. Changes in customer demand
8. Political conflict
9. Cybersecurity attacks
10. Changes in market prices

The project team may have little control over an external event, but it can still prepare a response.

## 3. Why Risk Management Matters

Risk management helps the project team prepare before problems occur.

Without proper risk management, a project may experience:

1. Unexpected costs
2. Missed deadlines
3. Reduced quality
4. Injuries
5. Resource shortages
6. Customer dissatisfaction
7. Legal problems
8. Project failure

Risk management does not remove all uncertainty. Its purpose is to make uncertainty visible, understandable, and manageable.

A proactive project team identifies risks early and develops responses before action becomes urgent.

## 4. Main Risk Response Strategies

The four main strategies for responding to negative risks are:

1. Avoid
2. Mitigate
3. Transfer
4. Accept

A fifth response, escalation, may be used when the project team does not have the authority or resources to manage the risk.

## 5. Risk Avoidance

Risk avoidance means removing the threat or changing the project so the threat can no longer affect it.

The team may:

1. Remove a dangerous activity
2. Use a safer material
3. Change the project scope
4. Select a proven technology
5. Extend the schedule
6. Cancel a risky part of the project

### Example

A renovation project discovers that an old wall may contain asbestos.

The team could avoid the risk by leaving the wall undisturbed or changing the renovation design so the wall does not need to be removed.

Avoidance is useful when the possible harm is too serious to justify continuing with the original plan.

## 6. Risk Mitigation

Risk mitigation means reducing the probability that a threat will occur or reducing its impact if it does occur.

The risk still exists, but the team makes it less dangerous or less damaging.

The team may:

1. Provide safety training
2. Add protective equipment
3. Test a system before launch
4. Create backups
5. Add schedule time
6. Divide work among several teams
7. Perform regular inspections
8. Improve quality controls

### Example

If workers must briefly enter an area that may contain hazardous material, the project could reduce exposure by using trained workers, limiting the time spent in the area, wearing proper protective equipment, and following an approved safety procedure.

Mitigation does not mean ignoring the risk. It means actively reducing its probability or impact.

## 7. Risk Transfer

Risk transfer means shifting responsibility for managing the threat to another party.

The original organization may still experience some consequences, but another party becomes responsible for part of the financial or operational risk.

Common transfer methods include:

1. Insurance
2. Contracts
3. Warranties
4. Guarantees
5. Outsourcing
6. Hiring a specialized contractor

### Example

A renovation company may hire a certified asbestos removal contractor. The contractor has specialized training, equipment, procedures, and insurance.

The danger does not disappear. However, responsibility for performing the hazardous work is transferred to a qualified organization.

Insurance is another form of transfer because the insurer agrees to cover specified financial losses.

## 8. Risk Acceptance

Risk acceptance means recognizing the risk without trying to avoid, transfer, or significantly reduce it.

Acceptance may be reasonable when:

1. The risk has a low probability
2. The impact is small
3. A response would cost more than the potential loss
4. The risk is outside the team’s control
5. No practical response is available

There are two forms of acceptance.

### Passive acceptance

The team records the risk but takes no immediate action.

### Active acceptance

The team prepares a contingency plan, reserve, or backup response in case the risk occurs.

### Example

A team may accept the possibility of a short weather delay but reserve two additional working days in the schedule.

Acceptance should be a deliberate decision. It should not result from forgetting or ignoring the risk.

## 9. Risk Escalation

A risk should be escalated when the project team cannot manage it because it lacks authority, resources, knowledge, or decision making power.

The risk may be sent to:

1. The project sponsor
2. Senior management
3. A steering committee
4. A regulatory department
5. Another program or department

### Example

A project manager discovers that a proposed system may violate an organization wide privacy requirement. The project manager cannot change the policy and should escalate the matter to senior management and the organization’s privacy specialists.

## 10. Responses to Positive Risks

Positive risks are opportunities that could improve project results.

The main responses to opportunities are:

1. Exploit
2. Enhance
3. Share
4. Accept
5. Escalate

### Exploit

Exploit means taking action to make sure the opportunity occurs.

Example: Assigning the most experienced team members to a valuable new product feature to ensure it is completed.

### Enhance

Enhance means increasing the probability or benefit of the opportunity.

Example: Increasing advertising to improve the chance that more customers will use a new service.

### Share

Share means working with another party that is better able to capture the opportunity.

Example: Creating a partnership with a company that has access to a larger market.

### Accept

Accept means taking advantage of the opportunity if it occurs without actively trying to make it happen.

### Escalate

Escalate means sending the opportunity to a higher level when its possible benefit exceeds the project manager’s authority or project boundaries.

## 11. Asbestos Safety Example

Asbestos was widely used in older construction materials because it resisted heat and fire. It can be found in materials such as insulation, ceiling tiles, pipe coverings, cement products, and some wall materials.

Asbestos becomes dangerous when its fibres are released into the air and inhaled. Exposure can cause serious diseases, including lung cancer, mesothelioma, and asbestosis.

A normal dust mask does not make asbestos work safe. Suspected asbestos should not be cut, drilled, sanded, broken, or removed by untrained workers.

A responsible project response may include:

1. Stop work in the affected area
2. Prevent people from disturbing the material
3. Arrange professional testing
4. Follow applicable workplace safety requirements
5. Hire qualified asbestos professionals when required
6. Use proper containment and protective equipment
7. Document the work and disposal process

Avoiding or transferring the work is often more appropriate than simply accepting the risk.

Safety risks must be treated differently from small cost or schedule risks. A limited project budget does not justify exposing workers to a serious health hazard.

## 12. Risk Appetite

Risk appetite is the general amount and type of uncertainty that an organization is willing to accept while pursuing its objectives.

Some organizations are cautious and prefer predictable results. Others are willing to accept more uncertainty in exchange for a greater possible reward.

### Example

A new technology company may accept a high level of product development risk to enter the market quickly.

A hospital may accept some schedule risk but have almost no appetite for patient safety risk.

Risk appetite can vary by category. An organization may accept financial uncertainty while refusing significant legal, safety, or privacy risk.

## 13. Risk Capacity

Risk capacity is the maximum amount of risk that an organization can absorb without threatening its survival or ability to operate.

Capacity is an absolute limit, not a preferred amount.

### Example

A company may be financially able to survive a loss of one million dollars. That amount represents its risk capacity.

However, the company may decide that it is only willing to place eight hundred thousand dollars at risk. That lower amount reflects its appetite.

Risk capacity should normally be greater than risk appetite.

## 14. Risk Tolerance

Risk tolerance describes the acceptable range of variation around a project objective.

Tolerance is more specific than risk appetite.

### Example

A project may have the following tolerances:

1. Schedule variation of up to five working days
2. Cost variation of up to five percent
3. No tolerance for serious safety incidents
4. System downtime of no more than two hours

Different stakeholders may have different tolerances. A sponsor may accept additional cost but refuse to delay the launch date.

## 15. Risk Threshold

A risk threshold is a defined point or condition that triggers action.

Once the threshold is reached, the team must respond, escalate, or activate a contingency plan.

### Example

A project has a cost tolerance of up to five percent. The project plan may state that a forecasted cost increase above three percent triggers a management review.

The three percent point is the threshold for action.

A threshold should be specific enough for the team to recognize when it has been reached.

## 16. Comparing Capacity, Appetite, Tolerance, and Threshold

### Risk capacity

The maximum amount of risk the organization can survive.

### Risk appetite

The general amount of uncertainty the organization is willing to accept for a possible reward.

### Risk tolerance

The acceptable range of variation around a specific objective.

### Risk threshold

The point that triggers a response, escalation, or contingency plan.

### Worked example

A company can survive a maximum project loss of one million dollars.

Its risk capacity is one million dollars.

The company is willing to place no more than eight hundred thousand dollars at risk.

Its risk appetite for the project is eight hundred thousand dollars.

The company considers cost changes within one hundred thousand dollars acceptable.

Its cost tolerance is one hundred thousand dollars.

The project plan requires action when forecasted losses reach seventy five thousand dollars.

Its risk threshold is seventy five thousand dollars.

## 17. Risk Management Process

A practical project risk management process contains the following stages:

1. Plan risk management
2. Identify risks
3. Perform qualitative risk analysis
4. Perform quantitative risk analysis when appropriate
5. Plan risk responses
6. Implement risk responses
7. Monitor risks

Risk management continues throughout the project. It is not completed by creating one document at the beginning.

## 18. Plan Risk Management

The risk management plan explains how the project team will manage uncertainty.

It may define:

1. The risk management approach
2. Roles and responsibilities
3. Risk categories
4. Probability and impact scales
5. Reporting methods
6. Meeting schedules
7. Risk appetite and thresholds
8. Response planning rules
9. Reserve management
10. Monitoring procedures

The plan does not list every individual risk. Those risks are normally recorded in the risk register.

## 19. Identify Risks

Risk identification asks:

What uncertain events or conditions could affect the project?

Sources of risk information may include:

1. Project documents
2. Contracts
3. Requirements
4. Assumptions
5. Estimates
6. Schedules
7. Lessons from previous projects
8. Interviews
9. Workshops
10. Expert judgement
11. Checklists
12. Cause and effect diagrams

The team should include people with different perspectives. Developers, testers, customers, suppliers, safety specialists, managers, and users may recognize different risks.

## 20. Writing a Clear Risk Statement

A useful risk statement connects the cause, uncertain event, and possible effect.

A simple structure is:

Because of a cause, an uncertain event may occur, which could produce an effect on the project.

### Weak risk statement

The developer is a risk.

This statement does not explain the uncertainty or its effect.

### Better risk statement

Because the only available developer may be unavailable next week, integration work may be delayed, which could postpone system testing.

This statement identifies:

1. Cause: the project depends on one developer
2. Risk event: the developer may become unavailable
3. Effect: integration and testing may be delayed

## 21. Risk Breakdown Structure

A Risk Breakdown Structure organizes risks into categories.

It is similar to a Work Breakdown Structure, but it organizes sources of uncertainty rather than project work.

Common categories include:

1. Technical risks
2. Schedule risks
3. Cost risks
4. Resource risks
5. Supplier risks
6. Legal risks
7. Safety risks
8. Environmental risks
9. Organizational risks
10. External risks

### Example for moving to a new home

#### Packing risks

1. Glass objects may break
2. Sharp objects may cause cuts
3. Boxes may become too heavy
4. Important items may be misplaced

#### Transportation risks

1. The vehicle may be too small
2. The truck may be damaged
3. Bad weather may delay the move
4. Items may move during transportation

#### Unpacking risks

1. Furniture may not fit
2. Boxes may be delivered to the wrong room
3. Important equipment may be damaged
4. People may be injured while lifting

The structure helps the team identify risks systematically instead of relying only on memory.

## 22. Qualitative Risk Analysis

Qualitative risk analysis prioritizes risks using descriptive or numbered ratings.

The two main factors are:

1. Probability
2. Impact

Probability describes how likely the risk is to occur.

Impact describes how seriously the project would be affected if the risk occurred.

A common scale is:

1. Very low
2. Low
3. Medium
4. High
5. Very high

The project should define what each rating means.

### Example probability scale

1. Very low: less than ten percent
2. Low: ten to thirty percent
3. Medium: thirty one to fifty percent
4. High: fifty one to seventy percent
5. Very high: more than seventy percent

### Example cost impact scale

1. Very low: less than one thousand dollars
2. Low: one thousand to five thousand dollars
3. Medium: five thousand one to twenty thousand dollars
4. High: twenty thousand one to fifty thousand dollars
5. Very high: more than fifty thousand dollars

The exact ranges should match the size and needs of the project.

## 23. Probability and Impact Matrix

A probability and impact matrix combines the probability and impact ratings.

A simple risk score can be calculated as:

Risk score = Probability rating × Impact rating

### Example

A risk has:

Probability rating = 4

Impact rating = 5

Risk score = 4 × 5 = 20

This would normally be treated as a high priority risk.

Another risk has:

Probability rating = 2

Impact rating = 2

Risk score = 2 × 2 = 4

This would normally receive less attention.

The project team should define score ranges before using the matrix.

### Example rating ranges

1. Scores from 1 to 5: Low priority
2. Scores from 6 to 14: Medium priority
3. Scores from 15 to 25: High priority

Multiplication is useful for ranking, but it does not replace judgement. A rare safety event with catastrophic consequences may require action even if its calculated score is not the highest.

## 24. Risk Urgency

Two risks can have similar scores but require different response times.

Risk urgency considers:

1. How soon the risk may occur
2. How much warning the team will receive
3. How long a response takes to prepare
4. Whether the effect can be reversed
5. Whether delayed action will make the risk worse

A moderate risk that may occur tomorrow might need attention before a high risk that cannot occur for six months.

## 25. Quantitative Risk Analysis

Quantitative risk analysis uses numerical information to estimate the possible effect of risk on cost, schedule, or other objectives.

Common methods include:

1. Expected monetary value
2. Decision tree analysis
3. Sensitivity analysis
4. Simulation
5. Statistical modelling
6. Expert estimates
7. Historical industry data

Quantitative analysis may require significant time, data, and expertise. It is most useful for large, expensive, complex, or highly uncertain projects.

Small projects may rely mainly on qualitative analysis when detailed numerical work would cost more than the value it provides.

## 26. Expected Monetary Value

Expected monetary value combines the probability of a risk with its financial impact.

The basic calculation is:

Expected monetary value = Probability × Financial impact

### Example

There is a thirty percent chance that equipment failure will cost forty thousand dollars.

Expected monetary value = 0.30 × $40,000

Expected monetary value = $12,000

This does not mean the project will definitely lose twelve thousand dollars. The actual result may be no loss or a forty thousand dollar loss.

The value helps the organization compare risks and estimate contingency reserves across many uncertain events.

Positive opportunities can have positive expected values. Threats normally have negative expected values.

## 27. Risk Register

The risk register is the main document used to record and track individual project risks.

A useful risk register may include:

1. Risk identification number
2. Risk description
3. Category
4. Cause
5. Possible effect
6. Probability
7. Impact
8. Risk score
9. Priority
10. Risk owner
11. Response strategy
12. Planned actions
13. Trigger
14. Contingency plan
15. Current status
16. Review date
17. Residual risk
18. Secondary risk

The risk register should be updated throughout the project.

## 28. Risk Owner

A risk owner is the person responsible for monitoring a risk and making sure the planned response is carried out.

The risk owner does not need to perform every response activity personally.

The owner should:

1. Watch for changes in probability or impact
2. Monitor warning signs
3. Report significant changes
4. begin the approved response when needed
5. Update the risk register
6. Confirm whether the response was effective

Every important risk should have a clearly named owner.

## 29. Risk Triggers

A trigger is a warning sign or condition showing that a risk is becoming more likely or that a response should begin.

### Example

Risk: The campus may not provide the final room list before testing.

Possible triggers include:

1. No room list received fourteen days before testing
2. No response from the campus contact after two requests
3. The sample data has not been replaced seven days before launch

A trigger should be observable and specific.

## 30. Contingency Plans

A contingency plan explains what the team will do if a particular risk occurs.

### Example

Risk: The final room list may arrive late.

Contingency plan:

1. Continue testing with clearly marked sample rooms
2. Prevent sample bookings from being treated as real reservations
3. Display a testing notice to users
4. Import the verified room list when received
5. Repeat critical booking tests before public launch

A contingency plan should identify the required actions, people, resources, and authority.

## 31. Fallback Plans

A fallback plan is used when the original response or contingency plan does not work.

### Example

The project plans to import the final room list before launch. If the list remains unavailable and the launch date cannot move, the fallback plan may be to release only the features that do not create real room reservations.

Fallback planning is especially useful for high priority risks.

## 32. Contingency Reserves

A contingency reserve is time, money, or another resource set aside for identified risks.

Examples include:

1. Extra funds for repair work
2. Additional testing time
3. Replacement equipment
4. Backup employees
5. Additional cloud computing capacity

The amount should reflect the project’s identified uncertainty.

Unused contingency funds are not extra profit for the project team. Their use should follow the organization’s approval and reporting procedures.

## 33. Residual Risks

A residual risk remains after a response has been implemented.

### Example

Hiring an experienced contractor may reduce the probability of installation errors, but it cannot guarantee that no error will occur.

The remaining possibility is the residual risk.

The team should decide whether the residual risk is acceptable or requires another response.

## 34. Secondary Risks

A secondary risk is a new risk created by a risk response.

### Example

The team hires an external contractor to reduce technical risk.

This response creates new risks involving:

1. Vendor communication
2. Contract disputes
3. Confidential information
4. Dependence on an external schedule
5. Integration problems

Risk responses should therefore be evaluated for the new risks they may introduce.

## 35. Monitoring and Controlling Risks

Risk management continues while the project is active.

The team should regularly:

1. Review existing risks
2. Identify new risks
3. Close risks that can no longer occur
4. Update probability and impact ratings
5. Check whether triggers have occurred
6. Confirm that responses are working
7. Review residual and secondary risks
8. Update contingency reserves
9. Report major changes
10. Escalate risks when required

A risk related to a completed activity can usually be closed.

For example, once kitchen packing is finished, the risk of being cut while packing kitchen knives is no longer active. The register should show that the risk is closed rather than leaving it as an active priority.

## 36. Risk Audits

A risk audit examines whether the project’s risk management process and responses are effective.

Questions may include:

1. Were important risks identified?
2. Were risk owners assigned?
3. Were agreed responses completed?
4. Did the responses reduce probability or impact?
5. Were warning signs noticed early?
6. Did the team update the risk register?
7. Did new risks appear?
8. Were contingency reserves used properly?

The purpose is to improve risk management, not simply to blame people.

## 37. Risk and Change Management

When a risk occurs, the project may need a formal change.

A risk response could affect:

1. Scope
2. Schedule
3. Budget
4. Staffing
5. Technology
6. Quality requirements
7. Contracts
8. Project objectives

Significant changes should follow the project’s change control process.

The project team should document:

1. What happened
2. Why a change is needed
3. The effect on the project
4. Available options
5. The recommended action
6. Required approval

Risk management and change management are connected, but they are not the same. Risk management prepares for uncertainty. Change management evaluates and controls proposed changes to the approved project plan.

## 38. Simple Moving Project Example

Imagine a family is moving to a new home.

### Risk 1

Risk: A person may be cut while packing kitchen knives.

Probability: Medium

Impact: Medium

Response: Mitigate

Action: Use protective knife covers and place the knives in a small, clearly labelled container.

### Risk 2

Risk: Cracked glasses may break during transportation.

Probability: High

Impact: Low

Response: Avoid

Action: Discard cracked glasses before packing.

### Risk 3

Risk: Furniture may be damaged during the move.

Probability: Medium

Impact: Medium

Response: Mitigate or transfer

Action: Use protective wrapping and hire insured movers.

### Risk 4

Risk: The rental truck may be involved in a collision.

Probability: Low

Impact: High

Response: Transfer and mitigate

Action: Purchase suitable rental insurance and use an experienced driver.

### Risk 5

Risk: Rain may delay the move.

Probability: Medium

Impact: Low

Response: Accept actively

Action: Keep covers available and reserve additional time.

This shows that risk management is used in everyday projects even when no formal chart is created.

## 39. Software Room Booking Project Example

Imagine a team is developing a campus room booking system.

The final list of bookable rooms has not been provided, one developer may be unavailable, and the launch date cannot be delayed.

### Risk 1: Final room list arrives late

Cause: The campus has not confirmed the room data.

Event: The final list may not arrive before testing.

Effect: The system may be tested with incomplete or incorrect room information.

Possible response: Mitigate

Actions:

1. Request the list by a clear deadline
2. Use labelled sample data for early testing
3. Design the import process so room data can be replaced quickly
4. Schedule a final test after receiving the verified list

Trigger: The list has not arrived fourteen days before launch.

Contingency plan: Limit the initial release to features that do not create real reservations.

### Risk 2: Developer becomes unavailable

Cause: Important knowledge is concentrated with one developer.

Event: The developer may be unavailable next week.

Effect: Development and issue resolution may be delayed.

Possible response: Mitigate

Actions:

1. Begin the critical work immediately
2. Document the design and code
3. Pair the developer with another team member
4. Transfer knowledge before the absence
5. Identify another person who can provide support

Trigger: The developer confirms the absence or becomes unavailable.

Contingency plan: Reassign essential work and postpone lower priority features.

### Risk 3: Users misunderstand sample rooms

Cause: Test data resembles real room information.

Event: Users may treat sample rooms as bookable locations.

Effect: Users may arrive at rooms that were never reserved.

Possible response: Mitigate

Actions:

1. Clearly label all sample data
2. Display a visible testing notice
3. Prevent confirmation messages from implying that a real booking exists
4. Restrict the pilot to selected testers
5. Provide instructions before testing begins

Trigger: A tester asks whether a sample reservation is real.

Contingency plan: Disable room booking and continue testing only the interface and workflow.

### Risk 4: Launch cannot be delayed

Cause: The launch date is fixed.

Event: Required data or features may remain incomplete.

Effect: The system may launch with limited functionality or confusing information.

Possible response: Mitigate and accept

Actions:

1. Define the minimum acceptable release
2. Remove incomplete features from public access
3. Communicate known limitations
4. Prepare a rapid update after launch
5. Assign staff to monitor user feedback

A warning banner alone does not transfer the risk. It is a communication and mitigation action. The project team remains responsible for managing the system.

## 41. Common Mistakes

### Treating an issue as a risk

If the event has already happened, it is an issue.

### Recording vague risks

Statements such as “technology risk” do not explain the event or its effect.

### Ignoring positive risks

Opportunities should also be identified and managed.

### Assuming insurance removes the risk

Insurance may transfer some financial consequences, but it does not remove safety, schedule, quality, or reputation effects.

### Using acceptance as an excuse

Acceptance should be a reasoned decision, not a substitute for planning.

### Focusing only on the score

A risk matrix supports judgement. It does not replace safety requirements, legal duties, or expert knowledge.

### Failing to assign an owner

A response is unlikely to happen if nobody is responsible for it.

### Never updating the register

Risks change as the project progresses. A register that is not updated quickly becomes unreliable.

### Confusing transfer with communication

Warning users about a risk does not transfer the responsibility to them.

### Spending too much on analysis

Risk analysis should match the size, complexity, and importance of the project.

## 43. Overall Summary

Every project contains uncertainty. Risks may come from internal or external sources and may create threats or opportunities.

Negative risks can be avoided, mitigated, transferred, accepted, or escalated. Positive risks can be exploited, enhanced, shared, accepted, or escalated.

Risk appetite describes how much uncertainty an organization is generally willing to accept. Risk capacity is the maximum it can absorb. Risk tolerance defines an acceptable range around an objective. A risk threshold identifies the point at which action is required.

The project team should plan how risks will be managed, identify uncertain events, evaluate their probability and impact, develop responses, assign owners, and monitor changes throughout the project.

The risk register records this information and should remain active throughout the project. It should be updated when risks change, new risks appear, responses are completed, or activities finish.

Good risk management is practical and proportional. It gives the most attention to risks that could seriously affect project objectives while avoiding unnecessary analysis for minor risks.
