# Lecture Notes: Procurement Management and Contract Types

## 1. What Is Procurement Management?

**Procurement management** is the process of acquiring products or services from an external source.

An external source could be:

- A supplier
- A contractor
- A consultant
- A software service provider
- Another organization outside the company

Procurement does not include products or services provided internally by the project team or organization.

Because procurement involves contracts, financial commitments, and legal obligations, it can have serious legal and financial consequences.

---

## 2. Procurement Management Planning

A procurement management plan identifies what the project must obtain from external sources and explains how those purchases will be managed.

The plan may include:

- The products or services that must be purchased
- The types of contracts that will be used
- Planned delivery dates
- Standard contract documents
- The number of suppliers or contractors involved
- The effect of purchasing decisions on project constraints
- Assumptions included in the project plan
- Coordination of purchasing activities
- Identification of qualified sellers
- Make-or-buy decisions

The purpose of procurement planning is to make sure the project gets the required products and services at the right time, cost, and quality.

---

# 3. Make-or-Buy Decisions

A **make-or-buy decision** determines whether an organization should create something internally or purchase it from an external supplier.

For example, imagine that a software company needs a time-tracking tool. The company could:

1. Build its own time-tracking system.
2. Purchase an existing tool or subscription.

The company may have the technical skills to build the system. However, that does not automatically mean that building it is the best decision.

## Main Principle

> Do not build something internally unless it is part of your core business.

If the product or service is not central to the organization’s main business, it may be better to purchase it from a specialized supplier.

Even if the organization has the required knowledge and capacity, building the product internally may create additional responsibilities, such as:

- Development
- Maintenance
- Updates
- Technical support
- Security
- Testing
- Long-term management

These activities can distract the organization from its core work.

## Common Startup Mistake

Startup companies sometimes try to do too many things internally because they have the skills to do them.

This creates several problems:

- Employees become divided among too many activities.
- The company loses focus.
- Products may not be completed properly.
- Maintenance becomes difficult.
- Resources are stretched across unrelated projects.

The main lesson is to focus on the organization’s core business and allow specialized suppliers to handle non-core products and services.

# 4. Main Types of Contracts

There are three main types of contracts:

1. **Fixed-price contracts**
2. **Cost-reimbursable contracts**
3. **Time-and-materials contracts**

Each contract type distributes cost and risk differently between the client and the contractor.

Understanding these differences is important because choosing the wrong contract can lead to cost overruns, delays, disagreements, or poor-quality work.

---

# 5. Fixed-Price Contracts

In a **fixed-price contract**, the client and contractor agree on:

- The work to be completed
- The required features or deliverables
- The completion time
- The total price

Once these conditions are agreed upon, the contractor is responsible for completing the work for the agreed price.

## Basic Example

A contractor agrees to complete a project for $10,000.

If the contractor requires more labour or material than expected, the client still pays $10,000, as long as the scope does not change.

## Risk Distribution

Fixed-price contracts generally place more risk on the contractor.

For example, if:

- Material prices increase
- The work takes longer than expected
- More labour is required
- The contractor makes an estimating mistake

The contractor is normally responsible for the additional cost.

## Contractor Profit

The contractor’s profit depends on how efficiently the work is completed.

- Less effort or lower cost results in more profit.
- More effort or higher cost results in less profit.

This can create different interests between the client and the contractor.

### Client’s interest

The client usually wants:

- High quality
- Complete work
- Good materials
- Strong support
- Careful completion

### Contractor’s interest

The contractor may prefer:

- Lower costs
- Less effort
- Faster completion
- Higher profit

Because of this difference, quality requirements must be clearly stated in the contract.

---

## 6. The Importance of a Clear Scope

A fixed-price contract requires a **clear scope from the beginning**.

The scope should explain exactly:

- What work will be completed
- What materials will be used
- What quality level is required
- What features are included
- What is not included
- When the work must be completed
- How the completed work will be inspected

If the client changes the scope after the contract is signed, the contractor will usually charge an additional amount.

The contractor may charge a high price for changes because the client is already committed to working with that contractor.

### Example

A contractor agrees to paint a house for $5,000.

Later, the client requests:

- Additional rooms
- More colours
- A different type of paint
- Extra repair work

These requests are outside the original scope, so the contractor can charge additional fees.

## Main Lesson

> In a fixed-price contract, changes are usually expensive.

The client should define the project carefully before signing the contract and try to minimize changes during the project.

---

## 7. Contingency in Fixed-Price Contracts

Because the contractor carries more risk, the contractor may include a **contingency amount** in the price.

A contingency is an additional amount added to protect the contractor against unexpected costs.

For example, a contractor may expect the actual work to cost $9,000 but submit a price of $10,000 to cover possible problems.

This means fixed-price contracts can provide strong cost predictability for the client, but the contractor may charge more to account for risk.

---

## 8. Comparing Contractors

A client should not automatically accept the first offer.

Different contractors may provide very different:

- Prices
- Completion times
- Materials
- Quality levels
- Warranties
- Features
- Work methods

For example:

- Contractor A may offer to complete the work for $5,000.
- Contractor B may offer to complete it for $15,000.

The difference may be caused by quality, experience, included services, materials, or how each contractor understands the scope.

The lecture recommends obtaining **at least three offers** when possible.

The client must compare not only the price, but also exactly what each contractor will provide.

---

# 9. Types of Fixed-Price Contracts

## 9.1 Fixed Total Price

Under a **fixed total price contract**, the contractor provides everything needed to complete the work.

This may include:

- Labour
- Materials
- Equipment
- Transportation
- Other required services

The client pays one total amount and does not separately purchase the materials.

### Example

A contractor agrees to complete a renovation for one total price. The contractor purchases the materials, provides the labour, and completes the work.

This arrangement is convenient because the client pays one amount and the contractor manages the rest.

---

## 9.2 Fixed Labour Price with Client-Supplied Materials

In some cases, the contractor charges a fixed amount for labour, while the client purchases the materials.

This arrangement can be useful because the client can:

- Select the materials
- Control the quality
- Choose preferred brands
- Take advantage of discounts
- Avoid contractor markups on materials

The contractor then uses the materials provided by the client to complete the work.

However, the contract should clearly explain who is responsible if the materials are incorrect, delayed, damaged, or insufficient.

---

## 9.3 Door-to-Door Supply Contract

A door-to-door contract may be used when products are purchased from an overseas supplier.

Under this arrangement, the supplier may manage:

- Shipping from the factory
- International transportation
- Customs procedures
- Taxes and duties
- Local transportation
- Delivery to the client’s location

The client pays one agreed amount and waits for the goods to arrive.

This is convenient because the client does not need to manage each stage of transportation.

However, if the client changes the delivery location, the supplier may charge an additional amount because the original contract has changed.

---

## 9.4 Fixed-Price Contract with Price Adjustment

A **fixed-price contract with price adjustment** begins with an agreed price, but the price may change according to specific rules.

The adjustment may be based on factors such as:

- Inflation
- Changes in product prices
- Changes in material costs
- Long project duration
- Other agreed market conditions

The adjustment rules must be included in the contract.

This type of contract is useful when the project will take a long time and prices may change significantly during that period.

---

## 9.5 Fixed-Price Contract with Incentive Fee

A normal fixed-price contract may not motivate the contractor to provide anything beyond the minimum requirements.

The contractor already knows the payment amount, so additional effort may reduce the contractor’s profit.

A **fixed-price contract with incentive fee** provides an additional payment if the contractor achieves a specified result.

Incentives may be provided for:

- Finishing early
- Improving quality
- Reducing cost
- Exceeding a performance target
- Providing additional value

### Example

A contractor may receive a bonus for increasing the quality level from an agreed minimum to a higher target.

The incentive should be based on measurable conditions stated in the contract.

---

## 9.6 Fixed Unit Price Contract

A **fixed unit price contract** establishes a price for each unit of a product or service.

The total payment depends on the number of units ordered.

### Formula

**Total cost = Number of units × Agreed price per unit**

### Example

A university signs a one-year agreement with a supplier.

The contract states that each roll of tissue costs $10.

If the university orders 100 rolls:

**Total cost = 100 × $10 = $1,000**

This contract type is useful for products that are purchased repeatedly, such as:

- Cleaning supplies
- Washroom supplies
- Office supplies
- Consumable materials

The unit price normally remains fixed for an agreed period, such as one year. After that period, the parties may negotiate new prices.

---

# 10. Summary of Fixed-Price Contracts

All fixed-price contracts require a relatively clear scope.

## Main characteristics

- Cost predictability is generally high.
- Changes to the scope can be expensive.
- Much of the risk is transferred to the contractor.
- Contractors may include contingency amounts.
- Quality requirements must be clearly stated.
- The client should compare multiple qualified suppliers.
- Incentives are normally low unless an incentive clause is included.

---

# 11. Cost-Reimbursable Contracts

In a **cost-reimbursable contract**, the client pays the contractor for the allowable costs of performing the work.

The client may also pay an additional fee representing the contractor’s profit.

## Basic Structure

**Total payment = Allowable costs + Contractor fee**

For example, if a contractor works for 10 hours, the client pays for 10 hours.

If the work takes 15 hours, the client pays for 15 hours.

The project cost is therefore not fixed in advance.

## When Cost-Reimbursable Contracts Are Used

These contracts are useful when:

- The project scope is not fully known.
- The cost of the work is difficult to estimate.
- The project involves uncertainty.
- The work may change as the project develops.

## Risk Distribution

The client carries more cost risk because the client must pay the allowable costs.

If the work becomes more expensive, the client generally pays more.

For this reason, the client must monitor:

- Labour hours
- Material expenses
- Subcontractor costs
- Receipts
- Progress
- Allowable and unallowable expenses

---

# 12. Types of Cost-Reimbursable Contracts

## 12.1 Cost Plus Fixed Fee

Under a **cost plus fixed fee contract**, the client pays:

1. The allowable project costs
2. A fixed fee to the contractor

### Formula

**Total payment = Allowable costs + Fixed fee**

The contractor’s fee does not change simply because the project cost increases.

This gives the contractor less motivation to increase project costs compared with a percentage-based fee.

---

## 12.2 Cost Plus Percentage Fee

Under a **cost plus percentage fee contract**, the client pays:

1. The allowable project costs
2. A percentage of those costs as the contractor’s fee

### Formula

**Total payment = Allowable costs + Percentage of allowable costs**

### Example

If the allowable cost is $100,000 and the contractor receives 5 percent:

**Contractor fee = $100,000 × 5% = $5,000**

**Total payment = $105,000**

A major concern is that the contractor earns a larger fee when the total cost increases.

This gives the contractor a weak incentive to reduce costs.

---

## 12.3 Cost Plus Incentive Fee

A **cost plus incentive fee contract** provides an additional payment if the contractor achieves a specified target.

The incentive may focus on:

- Reducing costs
- Finishing early
- Increasing quality
- Meeting a performance target

This arrangement attempts to align the contractor’s interests with the client’s goals.

The incentive conditions should be measurable and agreed upon before the work begins.

---

## 12.4 Cost Plus Award Fee

A **cost plus award fee contract** provides an additional award based on performance.

The award may be related to:

- Overall performance
- Quality
- Schedule
- Cost management
- Client satisfaction

An incentive is generally linked to a clearly defined target. An award may involve an evaluation of the contractor’s overall performance.

The lecture connected these contracts to project simulations in which a project manager could receive an additional reward for finishing early or reducing costs.

---

# 13. Time-and-Materials Contracts

A **time-and-materials contract** pays the contractor for:

- The time spent working
- The materials used
- Other agreed costs

The contractor may charge an hourly, daily, or weekly labour rate.

The contractor usually provides:

- Timesheets
- Material receipts
- Expense records
- Progress information

## Basic Structure

**Total payment = Labour charges + Material costs + Other agreed charges**

## Example

A client pays a contractor:

- A fixed amount each week for labour
- The actual cost of materials purchased

The final cost depends on how long the work continues and how much material is used.

---

## 14. Risks of Time-and-Materials Contracts

Time-and-materials contracts can be highly risky for the client.

The contractor earns more when:

- More hours are worked
- The project takes longer
- More materials are used

This creates a possible conflict of interest because the client wants the project completed quickly, while a longer project may provide more income to the contractor.

If the project is not controlled properly, it can become an ongoing project with no clear completion date.

## When This Contract May Be Appropriate

Time-and-materials contracts may be useful when:

- The work is relatively small.
- The scope is uncertain.
- The project involves risk.
- The exact amount of work cannot be estimated.
- The client needs work to begin quickly.

The lecture emphasizes that this contract should usually be used for smaller activities. Using it for a large project without limits can lead to serious cost problems.

---

## 15. Not-to-Exceed Limit

A time-and-materials contract should include a **not-to-exceed amount**.

A not-to-exceed amount establishes the maximum budget that the contractor cannot exceed without approval.

### Example

The contract may state:

> The contractor will be paid for time and materials, but the total payment cannot exceed $50,000 without written approval.

This protects the client from unlimited spending.

The contract should also include:

- A budget limit
- A schedule
- Deliverables
- Progress reporting
- Approval requirements for additional work
- A process for managing changes

---

# 16. Contractor Experience: A Lesson from a Renovation Project

The lecturer described a renovation project that began as a fixed total price contract.

The contractor was responsible for:

- Materials
- Labour
- The complete project

At the beginning, the contractor provided:

- A list of required materials
- A description of the work
- An estimated price
- A professional-looking plan

However, the client began requesting changes during the project.

The contractor agreed to the changes but did not clearly explain:

- The additional cost
- The additional time
- The effect on the schedule
- The new deliverables

As the changes increased, the original fixed-price arrangement was replaced by a weekly payment plus material costs.

The contract had effectively changed into a time-and-materials contract.

## Problems That Followed

- No maximum budget was established.
- No clear completion date was provided.
- Deliverables were not clearly defined.
- Weekly productivity decreased.
- The project continued while the contractor kept receiving payment.
- The client could not predict the final cost.
- Some of the work later had to be changed.

Eventually, the client demanded a clear plan and timeline. When the contractor did not provide one, the work was stopped.

## Main Lessons

- Understand the contract type before work begins.
- Do not allow a fixed-price contract to become an open-ended time-and-materials contract without careful control.
- Document every change.
- Request a new estimate before approving additional work.
- Require a schedule and clearly defined deliverables.
- Set a not-to-exceed amount.
- Monitor progress and productivity.
- Stop the work if the contractor refuses to provide basic planning information.

# 17. Comparing the Three Main Contract Types

| Contract Type      | Scope Clarity       | Main Cost Risk    | Cost Predictability | Contractor Incentive                    |
| ------------------ | ------------------- | ----------------- | ------------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| Fixed price        | High                | Mostly contractor | High                | Finish efficiently and protect profit   |
| Cost reimbursable  | Medium or uncertain | Mostly client     | Lower               | Depends on the fee structure            |
| Time and materials | Low or uncertain    | Mostly client     | Low                 | Continue billing for time and materials |

## Simple Interpretation

### Fixed price

Best when:

- The scope is clear.
- Deliverables can be defined.
- The client wants predictable cost.

Main danger:

- Changes can become expensive.

### Cost reimbursable

Best when:

- The work is uncertain.
- The cost cannot be estimated accurately.
- The scope may develop during the project.

Main danger:

- The client carries the risk of increasing costs.

### Time and materials

Best when:

- The work is small.
- The scope is uncertain.
- The duration cannot be known in advance.

Main danger:

- The project may continue indefinitely unless limits and controls are established.

# 18. Procurement Process

The procurement process includes several major steps:

1. Select the appropriate relationship and contract type.
2. Prepare a request for proposal or request for quotation.
3. Request bids from suppliers.
4. Evaluate the bids.
5. Award and sign the contract.
6. Manage the contract.
7. Monitor quality and performance.
8. Evaluate and document contract changes.
9. Inspect the final products or services.
10. Close the contract.

---

# 19. Selecting the Contract Type

Before selecting a contract, the project manager should consider:

- How clear the scope is
- How much uncertainty exists
- The level of project risk
- The expected amount of work
- The importance of the purchase
- Whether the cost is known in advance
- The contractor’s skills
- The contractor’s experience
- The uniqueness of the required product or service
- The type of relationship needed

The contract type must match the characteristics of the project.

For example, a fixed-price contract is usually unsuitable when the scope is highly uncertain.

---

# 20. Request for Quotation and Request for Proposal

There are two common ways to request offers from suppliers:

1. **Request for Quotation, or RFQ**
2. **Request for Proposal, or RFP**

## Request for Quotation

An RFQ is used when:

- The product or service is clearly defined.
- Specifications are known.
- There is little variation among possible solutions.
- Price is the main factor.

### Example

An organization wants to purchase computers with specific technical requirements.

Because the specifications are already known, suppliers mainly need to provide a price.

The question is essentially:

> How much will you charge for this clearly defined product?

---

## Request for Proposal

An RFP is used when:

- Suppliers may propose different solutions.
- Quality is important.
- Technical approaches may vary.
- The schedule may vary.
- The product or service is complex.
- Price is not the only evaluation factor.

A proposal may explain:

- What the supplier will provide
- How the work will be completed
- How long the work will take
- The proposed quality
- The supplier’s technical approach
- The total price
- The required effort

The question is broader:

> What solution will you provide, how will you provide it, and how much will it cost?

---

# 21. Bidding and Supplier Evaluation

After receiving an RFQ or RFP, suppliers submit offers, quotations, bids, or proposals.

The client should evaluate both technical and financial factors.

## Technical Evaluation

A technical evaluation may consider:

- Proposed solution
- Quality
- Materials
- Compliance with specifications
- Work plan
- Schedule
- Technical skills
- Previous experience
- Capacity to complete the work
- Similar completed projects

## Financial Evaluation

A financial evaluation may consider:

- Total price
- Unit price
- Payment schedule
- Additional fees
- Long-term costs
- Change costs
- Cost of maintenance
- Financial risk

The cheapest offer is not automatically the best offer.

A low-cost contractor may create additional problems if the contractor lacks experience, capacity, or technical knowledge.

---

# 22. Checking the Supplier

Before awarding a contract, the client should investigate the supplier.

Important questions include:

- Does the supplier have relevant experience?
- Has the supplier completed similar projects?
- Does the supplier have enough workers and equipment?
- Is the supplier trustworthy?
- Does the supplier have a good reputation?
- Can previous clients provide references?
- Can the client inspect previous projects?
- Does the supplier clearly understand the scope?
- Can the supplier meet the required schedule and quality?

If possible, the client should:

- Speak with previous customers.
- Review similar completed projects.
- Visit previous project locations.
- Check the supplier’s references.
- Confirm the supplier’s technical capacity.

Choosing an inexperienced supplier only because the price is low can lead to delays, poor quality, disputes, and additional costs.

---

# 23. Awarding and Signing the Contract

After evaluating the bids, the client selects the supplier that best satisfies the project’s needs.

The selection should consider:

- Technical quality
- Financial value
- Experience
- Capacity
- Risk
- Schedule
- Compliance with requirements

The final agreement should be written and signed.

The contract should clearly describe:

- Scope
- Deliverables
- Price
- Payment conditions
- Schedule
- Materials
- Quality standards
- Responsibilities
- Inspection process
- Change process
- Completion conditions

---

# 24. Managing the Contract

Signing the contract does not complete the procurement process. The contract must also be managed.

Contract management includes:

- Confirming the work schedule
- Monitoring progress
- Checking quality
- Reviewing invoices
- Verifying labour hours
- Checking receipts
- Coordinating logistics
- Managing payments
- Recording changes
- Resolving problems
- Confirming the contractor follows the agreement

Any contract change should be documented.

A change record should explain:

- What changed
- Why it changed
- How much additional money is required
- How much additional time is required
- Who approved the change

Verbal agreements should be avoided because people may forget or later disagree about what was discussed.

---

# 25. Inspection and Contract Closure

Before closing the contract, the client must confirm that everything promised has been delivered.

The client should not accept the supplier’s work based only on trust.

The client should:

- Count delivered items.
- Inspect materials.
- Check quality.
- Compare the delivery with the specifications.
- Test the product when possible.
- Confirm that all deliverables are complete.
- Document missing, damaged, or incorrect items.

If every item cannot be inspected, the client may inspect a sample.

Large projects may have dedicated procurement or inspection specialists who evaluate suppliers and verify delivered materials.

A supplier may be rejected if the delivered product:

- Does not meet the required quality
- Does not follow the specifications
- Is incomplete
- Is damaged
- Uses the wrong materials

The contract should only be closed after the client confirms that the supplier has completed the agreed work.

---

# 26. Procurement and Corruption Risks

Procurement involves money, and large contracts can involve hundreds of thousands or millions of dollars.

This creates opportunities for corruption.

Corruption can occur at several stages of procurement.

## 26.1 Writing the Requirements

A person may write the proposal requirements so that only one preferred supplier can qualify.

For example, the requirements may be customized for a specific supplier’s product.

This prevents fair competition.

## 26.2 Evaluating Bids

A supplier may receive unfair treatment during the evaluation process.

Examples include:

- Ignoring a stronger proposal
- Giving unfair scores
- Selecting a supplier for personal reasons
- Accepting money or personal benefits
- Favouring a supplier without proper justification

Unsuccessful suppliers may complain and request an investigation or reevaluation.

## 26.3 Inspection

A supplier may deliver:

- Lower-quality materials
- Incorrect products
- Damaged items
- Products that do not meet specifications

The supplier may then attempt to influence an inspector to approve the delivery.

## Legal Consequences

Procurement decisions may be legally reviewed.

Serious corruption can lead to:

- Investigation
- Contract cancellation
- Financial penalties
- Loss of employment
- Criminal charges
- Imprisonment

For this reason, procurement activities must be transparent, documented, fair, and carefully monitored.

---

# 27. Quality Control in Procurement

Quality expectations should be included in the contract from the beginning.

The contract should not simply say that a contractor will provide materials. It should specify the expected:

- Type
- Brand, if required
- Model
- Grade
- Quantity
- Colour
- Size
- Performance level
- Quality standard

If the client does not define the required quality, the contractor may provide the cheapest available option.

### Example

For a painting project, the contract should identify the required type or quality of paint.

Without a clear requirement, the contractor may use inexpensive paint that:

- Looks poor
- Does not last
- Requires more maintenance
- Does not meet the client’s expectations

Clear specifications make inspection easier because the client can compare the delivered product directly with the written contract.

---

# 28. Importance of Written Agreements

Important project information should be written down.

This includes:

- Scope
- Price
- Materials
- Schedule
- Deliverables
- Changes
- Quality requirements
- Payment arrangements
- Responsibilities

A verbal agreement is difficult to prove and easy to misunderstand.

People may forget what they agreed to, either intentionally or unintentionally.

Even for a personal project, such as a home renovation, a written agreement protects both sides.

# 30. Exam Review Summary

## Three Main Contract Types

### Fixed Price

- The amount is agreed upon in advance.
- The contractor carries more cost risk.
- The scope must be clear.
- Changes usually cost more.
- Cost predictability is high.

### Cost Reimbursable

- The client pays allowable costs plus a contractor fee.
- It is useful when the scope or cost is uncertain.
- The client carries more cost risk.
- Expenses must be monitored carefully.

### Time and Materials

- The client pays for labour time and materials.
- It is useful for smaller work with uncertain scope.
- The client carries most of the risk.
- A not-to-exceed limit is important.
- Without control, the project may continue indefinitely.

## RFQ Versus RFP

### RFQ

- Used when requirements are clear.
- Focuses mainly on price.
- Suitable for standard products or services.

### RFP

- Used when different solutions are possible.
- Considers technical quality, schedule, approach, and price.
- Suitable for complex products or services.

## Final Principle

> The contract type determines who carries the risk.

A good procurement decision requires a clear scope, a suitable contract type, qualified suppliers, written agreements, careful monitoring, and proper inspection.
