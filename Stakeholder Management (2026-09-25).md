# COSC 305 Topic 5 Review: Stakeholder Management

## 1. What Is a Stakeholder?

A **stakeholder** is an individual or group that:

• cares about a project  
• is affected by a project  
• can affect the project  
• has an interest in the project's outcome

Stakeholders can exist both **inside and outside the organization**.

A **key stakeholder** is especially important because they have enough influence to significantly help or harm the project.

In extreme cases, a key stakeholder may have the ability to **make or break the project**.

### Why Stakeholder Management Matters

Failing to correctly identify and manage stakeholders can cause a project to fail.

As project complexity increases, a Project Manager, or **PM**, may have to interact with many stakeholders.

Poor stakeholder relationships can create problems with:

• resources  
• schedules  
• requirements  
• approvals  
• communication  
• cooperation  
• project acceptance

### Core Idea

**A technically successful project can still fail if its stakeholders are poorly managed.**

---

# 2. Examples of Project Stakeholders

A project can involve many different stakeholder groups.

Important examples include:

• senior management  
• project team members  
• the Project Manager's supervisor  
• peers  
• internal customers  
• external customers  
• resource managers  
• government  
• contractors  
• subcontractors  
• suppliers  
• subject matter experts  
• approval boards

Not every stakeholder has the same amount of influence or interest.

Therefore, the PM must determine:

1. Who are the stakeholders?
2. What do they want?
3. How much power do they have?
4. Do they support or oppose the project?
5. How should the PM communicate with them?

---

# 3. Stakeholder Interactions

Managing stakeholders can be difficult because the PM interacts with both:

• internal stakeholders  
• external stakeholders

External stakeholders can be especially difficult because the PM may have **little direct authority over them**.

### Example: Third Party Software Developer

Suppose a third party developer is responsible for creating an important software module.

If that module is on the project's **critical path**, late delivery could delay the entire project.

However, the PM may have little direct control over that developer.

Therefore:

external dependency
↓
limited PM authority
↓
possible delay
↓
critical path affected
↓
entire project may be delayed

This demonstrates why stakeholder management depends heavily on:

• relationships  
• influence  
• communication  
• negotiation

rather than authority alone.

---

# 4. Key Stakeholder: Senior Management

**Senior management** consists of people responsible for directing organizational strategy and development.

Examples include:

• company presidents  
• vice presidents  
• directors  
• division managers

Senior management can be extremely powerful stakeholders.

---

# 5. Advantages of Senior Management Support

A project supported by senior management may have an easier time obtaining:

• funding  
• equipment  
• employees  
• organizational support  
• approvals  
• other necessary resources

A successful project can benefit:

• the Project Manager  
• the project team  
• senior management  
• the organization

### Important Relationship

senior management support
↓
better access to organizational resources
↓
greater ability to execute the project
↓
increased chance of project success

---

# 6. Risks for Senior Management

High visibility creates risk.

If a large or expensive project fails:

• the failure may become highly visible  
• substantial money may be lost  
• organizational objectives may not be achieved  
• the failure may reflect badly on senior management

This means senior managers are often highly interested in major project performance.

The PM should understand this when deciding what information senior management needs.

---

# 7. Communicating With Senior Management

**Communication is essential.**

The PM should:

• develop detailed project plans  
• identify major milestones requiring approval  
• determine what information senior management expects  
• determine how frequently information should be reported  
• establish a consistent reporting methodology  
• report project risks openly  
• explain the potential effects of risks

A **dashboard summary** can be useful for regularly communicating project status.

### Key Principle

Senior management should not unexpectedly discover a major problem that the PM already knew about.

Important risks should be communicated early enough for management to respond.

---

# 8. Key Stakeholder: The Project Team

The project team consists of people working directly on project activities.

Team members may be:

• dedicated full time to the project  
• assigned only part time  
• temporarily borrowed from another department

The Project Manager provides leadership and direction to the team.

The PM should also support team members while they complete their work.

---

# 9. Why the PM Should Work Closely With the Team

Working closely with team members allows the PM to:

• understand problems sooner  
• learn from team expertise  
• establish rapport  
• identify risks  
• gain cooperation  
• provide support when needed

The PM should not simply assign work and disappear.

Supporting team members can encourage team members to support the PM and the project in return.

---

# 10. Potential Problems With Project Teams

Several problems can arise.

## Borrowed Team Members

A borrowed employee may not directly report to the PM.

They may have responsibilities to another manager.

Therefore, competing priorities may develop.

## Multiple Projects

A team member may simultaneously have:

• a full time job  
• this project  
• other projects

This can make deadlines difficult to meet.

## Personality Conflicts

Conflicts can arise because of differences in:

• personality  
• social style  
• values  
• working style  
• previous experiences

People who had a bad experience working together in the past may bring that conflict into the current project.

---

# 11. Building a Strong Project Team

The PM can strengthen the team by:

• involving team members in project planning  
• privately meeting with individual team members during the project  
• having informal conversations over lunch or coffee  
• being available when concerns arise  
• encouraging people to help one another  
• providing useful performance feedback  
• recognizing individual contributions

### Why Involve People in Planning?

People are generally more likely to support a plan when they have participated in creating it.

Participation can also reveal technical knowledge or operational problems that the PM does not personally understand.

---

# 12. Key Stakeholder: Your Manager or Supervisor

The Project Manager's own manager is another important stakeholder.

The manager may influence:

• which assignments the PM receives  
• who may work with the PM  
• what resources are available  
• the PM's authority  
• the PM's organizational support

Keeping the manager informed can improve the PM's ability to obtain necessary resources.

---

# 13. Why Manager Support Matters

Projects sometimes encounter serious problems.

When this occurs, an understanding and supportive manager can advocate for the PM.

A manager can potentially:

• obtain additional resources  
• resolve organizational disagreements  
• provide authority  
• protect project priorities  
• help escalate major problems

A lack of management support can severely damage a project.

---

# 14. Building a Relationship With Your Manager

A strong relationship requires:

• reciprocity  
• respect  
• clarification  
• communication  
• reliability

The PM should support their manager where appropriate.

The PM should also determine:

• how their performance will be measured  
• what their manager expects  
• what information their manager needs  
• how often reports should be provided

If instructions are unclear, **ask for clarification**.

A reporting schedule should be agreed upon and then consistently followed.

---

# 15. Key Stakeholder: Peers

**Peers** are people at approximately the same organizational level as the Project Manager.

They may or may not belong directly to the project team.

Peers can still influence the project.

They may have an interest in the project's outcome without having direct responsibility for its success or failure.

### Important Point

**Peer support can be critical to project success.**

---

# 16. Managing Peer Relationships

The PM may need to use:

• investigation  
• persuasion  
• influence  
• negotiation  
• organizational awareness

The PM does not necessarily have formal authority over peers.

Therefore, cooperation cannot always be commanded.

It often has to be earned or negotiated.

---

# 17. How to Gain Peer Support

The PM can:

• obtain visible support from the project sponsor or senior management  
• ensure the sponsor makes cooperation expectations clear  
• request appropriate authority where necessary  
• communicate directly with peers  
• explicitly ask for their support  
• clearly explain what is needed  
• confront dysfunctional behaviour appropriately  
• conduct regular review meetings  
• establish goals and performance standards

If someone is repeatedly speaking negatively about the project or creating dysfunctional behaviour, ignoring the problem may allow it to spread.

The PM should address the behaviour directly and professionally.

---

# 18. Key Stakeholder: Customers

Customers are particularly important because they may ultimately **accept or reject the project's work**.

There are two broad customer categories.

## Internal Customers

Individuals or groups inside the organization for whom the project is being completed.

## External Customers

People or organizations outside the company.

Examples include:

• customers purchasing the final product  
• clients paying for contract development

---

# 19. Managing Customer Expectations

The Project Manager should negotiate, clarify, and document:

• project requirements  
• specifications  
• deliverables  
• expectations

This should happen **early**.

The PM must continue monitoring customer concerns throughout the project.

### Why Early Clarification Matters

unclear expectations
↓
different interpretations
↓
different assumptions
↓
incorrect project output
↓
customer dissatisfaction
↓
possible rejection or rework

---

# 20. Common Problems With Customers

Potential problems include:

• unclear customer expectations  
• insufficient documentation  
• poor understanding of the customer's organization  
• unrealistic deadlines  
• unrealistic budgets  
• unrealistic specifications  
• customer unwillingness to approve decisions  
• customer unwillingness to accept responsibility for decisions  
• changing project scope

Many of these problems become much more expensive if discovered late.

---

# 21. How to Meet Customer Needs

The PM should learn the customer's:

• business  
• terminology  
• organizational culture  
• operating environment

Requirements and specifications should be clearly documented in a written agreement.

The project should also establish a clear **change procedure**.

This answers questions such as:

1. Who can request a change?
2. Who evaluates the change?
3. Who approves it?
4. What effect does it have on cost?
5. What effect does it have on schedule?
6. What effect does it have on scope?

Without a clear change process, uncontrolled changes can destabilize the project.

---

# 22. Single Point of Communication

The Project Manager should generally serve as a central communication point for project information.

Why?

Multiple uncontrolled communication paths can produce:

• conflicting instructions  
• inconsistent requirements  
• duplicated work  
• confusion  
• misunderstandings

A clear communication structure reduces mixed messages.

---

# 23. Other Stakeholders: Resource Managers

**Resource managers** control resources required by a project.

These resources may include:

• employees  
• specialists  
• equipment  
• facilities  
• technical expertise

The PM's relationship with resource managers is therefore important.

A poor relationship may make obtaining necessary resources more difficult.

---

# 24. Other Stakeholders: Government

Government stakeholders may exist at several levels:

• federal  
• provincial  
• municipal or civic  
• regional

Government stakeholders may affect projects through matters such as approvals, regulations, rules, or other requirements.

The specific importance of government depends on the nature of the project.

---

# 25. Other Stakeholders: Subcontractors

Subcontractors are often used when required expertise or capacity is unavailable internally.

A subcontractor relationship can become complicated enough that significant management effort is required.

Potential problems include:

• cost overruns  
• quality problems  
• schedule slippage

The relationship between the project organization and subcontractor is therefore extremely important.

---

# 26. Stakeholder Politics

Stakeholders can have **conflicting interests**.

One stakeholder may want:

• lower costs

while another wants:

• additional features

and another wants:

• the earliest possible completion

These objectives can conflict.

The Project Manager must understand and manage these competing expectations.

---

# 27. Project Managers and Political Influence

The lecture compares Project Managers to politicians.

This does not necessarily mean party politics.

The idea is that PMs often lack enough formal authority to simply impose every decision.

Instead, they must influence people.

A successful PM needs to:

• understand interests  
• negotiate  
• build support  
• resolve disagreements  
• create alliances  
• communicate effectively

### Core Idea

**Project management requires influence, not merely authority.**

---

# 28. Managing Stakeholder Conflict

The PM should identify and meet key stakeholders **early in the project**.

The objective is to understand:

• stakeholder needs  
• expectations  
• constraints  
• priorities  
• influence

The slides state that when stakeholder interests conflict and there is uncertainty, the conflict should generally be resolved in favour of the customer.

However, this should still be considered alongside the project's agreed scope, organizational objectives, contractual requirements, and decision authority.

---

# 29. Stakeholder Management Step 1: Assess the Environment

The first major step is identifying the relevant stakeholders.

Ask:

**Who cares about the project?**

**Who is affected?**

**Who can affect it?**

Then determine each stakeholder's interests.

The PM must understand who the stakeholder is and what they need.

---

# 30. Find Where the Power Lies

Once stakeholders have been identified, determine who has meaningful power.

Ask:

• Who matters most to this project?
• Who can change its outcome?
• Who controls necessary resources?
• Who can approve or reject work?
• Whose actions could seriously affect the project?

Not every stakeholder deserves identical management effort.

A stakeholder's influence helps determine how much attention the PM should give them.

---

# 31. Stakeholder Management Step 2: Identify Goals

For every important stakeholder, determine:

• What motivates them?
• What do they want?
• What benefits are they expecting?
• What goals are openly stated?
• Might there be unstated objectives?
• How important is the project to them?

Pay particularly close attention to stakeholders with substantial power.

### Essential Question

**What's in it for them?**

Understanding this makes stakeholder behaviour easier to predict and manage.

---

# 32. Stakeholder Management Step 3: Define the Problem

When a stakeholder issue develops, identify the actual facts.

Do not immediately react to:

• assumptions  
• rumours  
• interpretations  
• emotional reactions

Instead repeatedly ask:

**What is the real situation?**

The PM should isolate and closely examine the facts creating the problem.

---

# 33. Culture and Stakeholder Management

Stakeholders can have different cultural backgrounds and values.

Organizations and Project Managers may need to adapt their work processes accordingly.

Cultural differences can influence:

• negotiation  
• decision making  
• communication  
• language  
• context  
• directness  
• body language

Language can become a particularly significant communication barrier.

---

# 34. Communication Across Cultures

Effective project execution often requires information to be exchanged:

• quickly  
• accurately  
• clearly

Different cultural communication styles can affect how messages are interpreted.

For example, different cultures may have different expectations concerning:

• how directly disagreement should be expressed  
• how much contextual information is expected  
• who is expected to make decisions  
• how people communicate concerns

The PM should therefore avoid assuming that every stakeholder communicates in the same way.

---

# 35. Stakeholder Complexity

Projects often have more than one major stakeholder.

Every additional stakeholder can increase project complexity.

Complexity depends partly on:

• number of stakeholders  
• stakeholder influence  
• stakeholder investment in the project  
• emotional investment  
• business investment  
• ability to affect project outcomes  
• degree of agreement or disagreement

### Example

Many stakeholders who generally agree
=

manageable complexity

Many powerful stakeholders with conflicting objectives
=

much greater complexity

---

# 36. Building Strong Stakeholder Relationships: Analyze Stakeholders

Conduct a **stakeholder analysis**.

This means systematically examining the project's important participants.

Identify:

• individual characteristics  
• interests  
• motivations  
• possible sources of conflict  
• roles  
• expected participation  
• influence

The PM should particularly look for **conflicts of interest**.

---

# 37. Assess Stakeholder Influence

Determine how much influence each stakeholder has.

Generally:

**greater stakeholder influence → greater need for careful management**

But influence should also be considered alongside support.

For each stakeholder, ask:

• How powerful are they?
• How supportive are they?
• What do they want?
• What can they do to the project?
• What would increase their support?

Again, the central question is:

**What's in it for them?**

---

# 38. Understanding Expectations

Expectations should be identified **early** in the project.

The PM should:

• ask questions  
• request clarification  
• document important expectations  
• resolve ambiguity

Do not assume everyone defines project success the same way.

---

# 39. Define Project Success Early

Different stakeholders may have completely different definitions of success.

For example:

Customer:

> The system contains every feature I requested.

Senior management:

> The project stayed within budget.

Project team:

> The system is technically reliable.

Marketing:

> The product launched on time.

These definitions can conflict.

Therefore, success should be defined **before the project is completed**.

Stakeholder definitions of success should be understood and incorporated into project objectives where appropriate.

### Critical Idea

**Do not wait until the end of a project to discover what stakeholders expected from it.**

---

# 40. Stakeholder Involvement

Ask stakeholders to participate when their involvement is needed.

However, understand each person's:

• availability  
• workload  
• capacity  
• schedule

Stakeholder participation must be realistic.

Someone can be important without having unlimited time to participate.

Respecting stakeholder schedules can help maintain cooperation.

---

# 41. Stakeholder Communication

Provide regular and appropriate status updates.

The slides describe a **Goldilocks approach**:

not too many updates

not too few updates

just the appropriate amount

Weekly communication may be appropriate in many situations, although the correct frequency ultimately depends on the project and stakeholder.

The PM should also:

• schedule meetings when necessary  
• avoid excessive gaps between important meetings  
• answer stakeholder questions promptly  
• respond to important messages promptly

---

# 42. Different Stakeholders Require Different Approaches

A stakeholder analysis helps the PM determine how to interact with each stakeholder.

The PM should **not communicate identically with everyone**.

For every:

• conversation  
• report  
• email  
• meeting

think about the stakeholder's:

• needs  
• expectations  
• power  
• interests  
• preferred information

Even informal communication can affect stakeholder relationships.

---

# 43. Organizational Interests Come First

The slides emphasize:

**The organization's interests are more important than the personal interests of either the PM or an individual stakeholder.**

Stakeholder management is therefore not about giving every person whatever they personally want.

The PM must balance stakeholder needs against the objectives of the project and organization.

---

# 44. Characteristics of a Trusted Project Manager

Stakeholders are more likely to respect a Project Manager who:

• is honest  
• provides difficult information when necessary  
• takes ownership  
• behaves predictably  
• is reliable  
• stands behind decisions  
• accepts responsibility for mistakes

Trust is especially important when the project begins experiencing problems.

### Key Principle

**Supportive stakeholders are essential to project success.**

---

# 45. Stakeholder Analysis Tool: Step 1, Identify Stakeholders

The first step is identifying who genuinely needs to be considered.

Do not assume someone's importance based only on their own perception.

Someone may believe they are important but have little meaningful connection to the project.

Conversely, someone may think they do not need to participate even though their approval or expertise is essential.

Possible targets include:

• your manager  
• your manager's manager  
• client  
• client's manager  
• required Subject Matter Experts, or SMEs  
• review boards  
• approval boards

Stakeholder identification therefore requires careful judgment.

---

# 46. Stakeholder Analysis Tool: Step 2, Identify Power

For each stakeholder determine:

• What power do they have?
• Can they affect the project?
• What are their intentions?
• Do they support the project?
• Do they oppose the project?
• What strategy should the PM use with them?

Power matters because a highly influential opponent presents a different risk from a person with little influence.

---

# 47. Stakeholder Analysis Tool: Step 3, Identify Relationships

Stakeholders also influence **one another**.

Therefore, do not analyze each stakeholder completely in isolation.

Ask:

• What relationships exist among stakeholders?
• Who trusts whom?
• Who influences whom?
• Who supports the project?
• Who opposes it?
• Can supporters help improve the attitudes of opponents?

### Example

supportive senior manager
↓
influences department manager
↓
department manager provides employees
↓
project receives necessary resources

This demonstrates why the relationship network matters.

---

# 48. Stakeholder Analysis Tool: Step 4, Build the Analysis

After collecting information, organize it into a formal stakeholder analysis.

A useful analysis should capture information such as:

• stakeholder identity  
• project interest  
• level of influence  
• level of support  
• expectations  
• relationships  
• communication needs  
• potential risks  
• management strategy

Templates can be used to structure this information.

The important issue is not the particular template.

The purpose is to transform stakeholder information into a useful management strategy.

---

# 49. Stakeholder Analysis Tool: Step 5, Build the Communication Plan

Once stakeholders are understood, develop a **stakeholder communication plan**.

The plan should answer questions including:

1. Who needs information?
2. What information do they need?
3. Why do they need it?
4. How much detail is appropriate?
5. How should it be communicated?
6. How frequently should it be communicated?
7. Who is responsible for communicating it?
8. How should questions and feedback be handled?

### Core Principle

**Communication should be designed around the stakeholder, not simply around what is easiest for the Project Manager.**

---

# 50. Complete Stakeholder Management Process

The overall process can be remembered as:

Identify stakeholders  
↓  
Understand their interests  
↓  
Determine their power  
↓  
Determine support or opposition  
↓  
Understand stakeholder relationships  
↓  
Clarify goals and expectations  
↓  
Define problems accurately  
↓  
Determine appropriate involvement  
↓  
Develop relationship strategies  
↓  
Create communication plans  
↓  
Communicate and monitor continuously  
↓  
Adjust stakeholder strategy as circumstances change

---

# 51. Power and Support

Two particularly useful dimensions for thinking about stakeholders are:

**Power**

How strongly can this stakeholder affect the project?

**Support**

How strongly does this stakeholder support or oppose the project?

This produces four useful situations.

## High Power and High Support

These stakeholders are extremely valuable.

Maintain their support and keep them appropriately involved.

## High Power and Low Support

These stakeholders represent major project risks.

Understand why they oppose the project and actively manage the relationship.

## Low Power and High Support

They may not control the project but can still provide useful assistance and advocacy.

Keep them appropriately informed and involved.

## Low Power and Low Support

They normally require less management attention than highly influential stakeholders.

However, do not completely ignore them if their position or influence might change.

---

# 52. Communication Is the Connecting Theme

Communication appears throughout almost every stakeholder category in this topic.

## Senior Management

Provide structured reporting and communicate risks.

## Team

Listen, support, clarify, and remain available.

## Manager

Clarify expectations and establish reporting schedules.

## Peers

Be direct about what support is needed.

## Customers

Document requirements and manage changes.

## Resource Managers

Maintain relationships so necessary resources remain available.

## Subcontractors

Monitor cost, schedule, quality, and obligations.

## All Stakeholders

Understand expectations and communicate appropriately.

Therefore:

**Good stakeholder management is largely relationship management supported by effective communication.**

# 53. Common Causes of Stakeholder Problems

Stakeholder problems often originate from:

• failing to identify an important stakeholder  
• misunderstanding who has power  
• unclear requirements  
• undocumented expectations  
• conflicting definitions of success  
• poor communication  
• communicating too little  
• communicating too much  
• ignoring cultural differences  
• conflicting stakeholder goals  
• uncontrolled scope changes  
• resource competition  
• personality conflicts  
• unrealistic budgets  
• unrealistic schedules  
• unrealistic specifications  
• failure to confront dysfunctional behaviour  
• failing to understand stakeholder relationships

Many of these problems can be reduced through **early stakeholder analysis**.

# 54. High Value Exam Distinctions

## Stakeholder vs Key Stakeholder

**Stakeholder**

Someone interested in, affected by, or capable of affecting the project.

**Key Stakeholder**

Someone with enough influence or importance to significantly affect project success or failure.

---

## Internal vs External Customer

**Internal customer**

Exists inside the organization.

**External customer**

Exists outside the organization, such as a paying client or market customer.

---

## Authority vs Influence

**Authority**

Formal organizational power to direct someone.

**Influence**

Ability to affect someone's choices without necessarily having formal authority.

Project Managers frequently depend heavily on **influence**.

---

## Stakeholder Interest vs Stakeholder Power

**Interest**

How much a stakeholder cares about the project.

**Power**

How much the stakeholder can affect the project.

A person can have high interest but little power, or substantial power but relatively little interest.

---

# 55. Scenario Example: External Software Developer

A Project Manager depends on an external software developer for a critical module.

The developer is two weeks behind schedule.

The module is on the critical path.

The PM does not directly manage the developer's employees.

### Stakeholder Problem

External dependency combined with limited formal authority.

### Possible Effects

late module
↓
critical path delay
↓
project schedule delay
↓
possible cost increase
↓
customer dissatisfaction

### Appropriate PM Response

The PM should:

• communicate immediately  
• determine the actual cause  
• evaluate schedule impact  
• understand contractual obligations  
• work with the external stakeholder on recovery options  
• escalate appropriately if needed  
• communicate significant risk to affected stakeholders

### Main Lesson

**Stakeholder management becomes especially important when the PM has responsibility for an outcome without direct authority over everyone producing it.**

---

# 56. Scenario Example: Customer Scope Change

A customer requests several additional features midway through development.

### Problem

The customer is a powerful stakeholder, but the request changes the agreed project scope.

The PM should not simply say yes without analysis.

Instead:

change request
↓
document request
↓
analyze schedule impact
↓
analyze cost impact
↓
analyze resource impact
↓
obtain appropriate approval
↓
update project documents
↓
implement if approved

This demonstrates why the customer relationship should include a defined change procedure.

---

# 57. Scenario Example: Conflicting Stakeholders

Suppose:

Senior management wants the project completed quickly.

The customer wants additional features.

The project team wants more time to ensure quality.

The finance department wants lower costs.

Every stakeholder may have a legitimate objective.

The Project Manager must:

• identify each objective  
• determine stakeholder power  
• identify constraints  
• clarify agreed project priorities  
• communicate tradeoffs  
• negotiate where possible  
• escalate decisions when necessary

The PM cannot simply satisfy every request because the requests may be mutually incompatible.

---

# 58. Scenario Example: Powerful Opponent

Suppose a department manager controls employees your project needs but does not support the project.

This stakeholder has:

**High power + low support**

This is a dangerous combination.

The PM should determine:

• Why does the manager oppose the project?
• What does the manager potentially lose?
• What does the manager need?
• Can the project sponsor help?
• Can goals be aligned?
• Can misunderstanding be corrected?

Ignoring the stakeholder does not remove their power.

---

# 59. Most Important Principles

## Principle 1

**Identify stakeholders early.**

Missing an important stakeholder can create serious problems later.

## Principle 2

**Determine where the power lies.**

Not every stakeholder has equal influence.

## Principle 3

**Ask: "What's in it for them?"**

Understand stakeholder motivations.

## Principle 4

**Define success early.**

Different stakeholders may define success differently.

## Principle 5

**Communicate appropriately.**

Different stakeholders require different information.

## Principle 6

**Build relationships before problems occur.**

It is easier to obtain support during a crisis when trust already exists.

## Principle 7

**Document customer requirements.**

Unclear expectations frequently produce conflict.

## Principle 8

**Control change.**

Customer requests should follow an established change process.

## Principle 9

**Understand stakeholder relationships.**

Stakeholders influence one another.

## Principle 10

**Influence matters when authority is limited.**

A Project Manager often cannot simply order people to cooperate.

**22. Stakeholder management begins by identifying all relevant stakeholders.**

**23. The PM should determine where stakeholder power lies.**

**24. For each stakeholder, ask: "What's in it for them?"**

**25. When stakeholder problems occur, separate facts from assumptions and ask: "What is the real situation?"**

**26. Cultural differences can affect negotiation, communication, decision making, language, context, directness, and body language.**

**27. More stakeholders usually increase project complexity, especially when powerful stakeholders disagree.**

**28. Stakeholder analysis identifies interests, motivation, roles, influence, participation, and potential conflicts.**

**29. The more influential a stakeholder is, the more carefully their support normally needs to be managed.**

**30. Stakeholders may have very different definitions of project success, so success must be clarified early.**

**31. Stakeholder communication should use a Goldilocks approach: not too much and not too little.**

**32. Different stakeholders require different communication and engagement strategies.**

**33. Organizational and project interests should take priority over individual personal interests.**

**34. A respected PM is honest, reliable, accountable, predictable, and willing to take ownership.**

**35. Stakeholder analysis should identify stakeholders, determine their power, determine their support or opposition, and examine relationships among them.**

**36. Supportive stakeholders can sometimes influence stakeholders who oppose the project.**

**37. Stakeholder analysis should ultimately be converted into a stakeholder communication plan.**

**38. Responsibility without authority is a major reason stakeholder management is so important to Project Managers.**

**39. Good stakeholder management combines analysis, communication, influence, negotiation, trust, and relationship building.**

**40. The central idea of Topic 5 is that project success depends not only on managing tasks, schedules, and budgets, but also on correctly identifying, understanding, communicating with, and gaining support from the people who can influence the project.**
