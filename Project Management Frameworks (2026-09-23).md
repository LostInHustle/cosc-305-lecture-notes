# 1. What Is a Project Management Framework?

Project management is both:

- A **body of knowledge** supported by established methods and research.
- A **practical human skill** involving judgment, experience, leadership, communication, and teamwork.

Managing a project requires many interconnected activities:

- Defining project scope
- Developing schedules
- Preparing budgets
- Planning human resources
- Recruiting people
- Procuring materials and services
- Managing quality
- Communicating with stakeholders
- Identifying and responding to risks

A **project management framework** provides a structured method for performing these activities by defining:

- **What** should be done
- **When** it should be done
- **How** different project management activities interact

This lecture focuses primarily on:

1. **PMBOK (Project Management Body of Knowledge)**
2. **Agile Project Management (especially Scrum)**

# Part I: PMI and the PMBOK Framework

## 2. Project Management Organizations

Two major project management organizations are:

### PMI (Project Management Institute)

### IPMA (International Project Management Association)

Both organizations support the profession through:

- Developing standards and best practices
- Publishing professional guidance
- Providing educational resources and training
- Offering certifications
- Promoting project management knowledge globally

### Importance of PMI

PMI is one of the world's most recognized project management organizations. Many employers require or prefer PMI certifications.

---

## 3. The PMBOK Guide

**PMBOK** stands for **Project Management Body of Knowledge**.

The PMBOK Guide:

- Compiles recognized project management knowledge
- Describes recommended project management processes
- Is periodically updated
- Serves as a foundation for professional project management practice and certification

### Editions

The lecture notes reference:

- **PMBOK 8th Edition (2025)** as the current version
- **PMBOK 6th Edition (2017)** as the framework used throughout the course

---

## 4. Structure of PMBOK Sixth Edition

PMBOK 6 organizes project management using three major structures:

### Process Groups

1. Initiating
2. Planning
3. Executing
4. Monitoring and Controlling
5. Closing

### Knowledge Areas

1. Integration Management
2. Scope Management
3. Schedule Management
4. Cost Management
5. Quality Management
6. Resource Management
7. Communications Management
8. Risk Management
9. Procurement Management
10. Stakeholder Management

### Processes

- 49 project management processes

Each process belongs to:

- One **Process Group**
- One **Knowledge Area**

# Part II: The Five Process Groups

## 5. Initiating

Purpose:

- Define and authorize a project or project phase.

### Key Processes

- Develop Project Charter
- Identify Stakeholders

### Main Outputs

#### Project Charter

Formally authorizes the project.

#### Stakeholder Identification

Identifies individuals or groups that:

- May affect the project
- May be affected by the project

---

## 6. Planning

Planning is the **largest process group**, containing **24 processes**.

It is often the most demanding stage because most major decisions are made before execution begins.

### Major Planning Activities

#### Integration

- Develop Project Management Plan

#### Scope

- Plan Scope Management
- Collect Requirements
- Define Scope
- Create Work Breakdown Structure (WBS)

#### Schedule

- Define Activities
- Sequence Activities
- Estimate Durations
- Develop Schedule

#### Cost

- Estimate Costs
- Determine Budget

#### Quality

- Plan Quality Management

#### Resources

- Estimate Resource Requirements
- Organize Resources

#### Communications

- Plan Communications

#### Risk

- Identify Risks
- Analyze Risks
- Plan Risk Responses

#### Procurement

- Plan Procurement

#### Stakeholders

- Plan Stakeholder Engagement

### Planning the Planning

Before detailed planning begins, project managers determine:

- Who will participate
- What information is required
- Which stakeholders will be consulted
- Which methods will be used
- When planning activities will occur
- How results will be approved and documented

---

## 7. Executing

Executing contains **10 processes**.

Purpose:

- Carry out the project management plan.
- Produce project deliverables.

### Major Activities

- Direct and manage project work
- Acquire resources
- Manage project team
- Manage quality
- Conduct procurements
- Implement risk responses
- Manage communications
- Engage stakeholders

Execution is where actual deliverables are created.

---

## 8. Monitoring and Controlling

Contains **12 processes**.

Occurs simultaneously with execution.

Purpose:

- Measure performance
- Compare results with plans
- Make corrective adjustments

### Activities

- Monitor scope
- Monitor schedule
- Monitor costs
- Monitor quality
- Control changes
- Track risks
- Monitor communications
- Monitor resources
- Control procurement
- Monitor stakeholder engagement

### Key Idea

Execution and monitoring occur together:

- Work is performed
- Progress is measured
- Adjustments are made

---

## 9. Closing

Purpose:

- Formally complete a project or phase.

### Primary Process

- Close Project or Phase

### Activities

- Confirm deliverable acceptance
- Finalize contracts

## 10. Project Integration Management

Integration Management coordinates all other knowledge areas.

Its purpose is to ensure:

- Activities work together
- Plans remain aligned
- Changes are coordinated
- Decisions are evaluated across all areas
- The project remains unified

### Importance in Large Projects

Large projects may involve:

- Multiple project managers
- Contractors
- Suppliers
- External organizations
- Specialized functional managers

Integration Management ensures all these components remain coordinated.

---

> What work is included in the project and what work is excluded.

### Activities

- Collect requirements
- Define scope
- Prepare scope statement
- Create WBS
- Validate scope
- Control scope changes

### Scope Statement Should Define

- Deliverables
- Requirements
- Assumptions
- Constraints
- Boundaries

Once dependencies are identified:

# Part IV: Project Management Constraints

## 20. Balancing Project Constraints

Traditional project constraints:

- Time
- Cost
- Scope (or Quality)

Additional perspectives may include:

- Resources
- Quality
- Risk

### Relationship Between Constraints

Changing one constraint usually affects others.

#### Examples

| Change           | Possible Impact              |
| ---------------- | ---------------------------- |
| Increase Quality | More Time and/or Cost        |
| Reduce Schedule  | Higher Cost or Lower Quality |
| Increase Scope   | More Resources and Time      |
| Reduce Resources | More Delays and Risk         |

### Key Principle

Project management is about balancing constraints rather than maximizing only one.

# Part V: Waterfall Project Management

## 21. Definition of Waterfall

Waterfall is a largely sequential project life cycle.

### Typical Sequence

1. Project Selection
2. Project Initiation
3. Project Planning
4. Project Execution/Implementation
5. Project Closing

Projects generally move forward through these phases with limited feedback loops.

---

## 22. Advantages of Waterfall

Waterfall offers:

- Clear phase structure
- Strong documentation
- Detailed planning
- Defined responsibilities
- Predictable milestones
- Structured project control

### Best Applied When

- Requirements are stable
- Work is well understood
- Major changes are unlikely

---

## 23. Limitations of Waterfall

Waterfall requires extensive upfront planning.

Teams must estimate:

- Requirements
- Resources
- Materials
- Equipment
- Procurement needs
- Budget
- Schedule
- Dependencies

### Challenge

Future conditions are uncertain.

Changes later in the project may require major rework.

---

## 24. Waterfall in Construction

Construction is naturally sequential.

### Example Sequence

1. Site Preparation
2. Foundation
3. Columns and Structure
4. Floors
5. Plumbing and Electrical Systems
6. Finishing Work

Physical components are difficult and expensive to redesign after completion.

Therefore, construction typically aligns well with waterfall approaches.

# Part VI: Iterative and Incremental Project Management

## 25. Iterative Development

An iterative approach repeats a complete work cycle multiple times.

Each iteration may include:

- Planning
- Execution
- Monitoring
- Testing
- Delivery
- Review

The output of one iteration becomes the foundation for the next.

---

## 26. Iterative Development in Software

Software is highly adaptable and therefore well suited to iterative development.

### Example: Online Store System

#### Release 1

- Registration
- Login

#### Release 2

- Product Catalogue

#### Release 3

- Shopping Cart

#### Release 4

- Payment Processing

#### Release 5

- Order Tracking

#### Release 6

- Third-Party Login

Benefits:

- Early delivery
- Continuous improvement
- Easier adaptation to changing requirements

# Part VII: Agile Project Management

## 27. Agile as an Iterative Framework

Agile uses iterative and incremental development.

Iterations typically last:

- 1 to 4 weeks

Each iteration may include:

- Planning
- Development
- Testing
- Monitoring
- Demonstration
- Review
- Delivery

Each cycle should produce working results.

---

## 28. Agile Principles and Working Culture

Agile emphasizes:

- Collaboration
- Teamwork
- Flexibility
- Frequent delivery
- Customer involvement
- Continuous improvement
- Working products
- Rapid response to change
- Empowered teams
- Self-motivated contributors

### Agile Teams

Instead of strict top-down management, team members help decide:

- How work will be performed
- Which activities are selected
- Effort estimates
- Problem-solving approaches
- Task organization
- Process improvements

Success is strongly based on teamwork.

---

## 29. Agile Meetings and Activities

### Sprint Planning

Determines:

- Features to develop
- Work to complete
- Required skills
- Capacity
- Resource needs

### Daily Stand-Up

Short meeting (~15 minutes).

Typical discussion:

1. What was completed yesterday?
2. What will be done today?
3. What obstacles exist?

Purpose:

- Rapid coordination
- Early identification of issues

### Sprint Demonstration

Completed work is:

- Demonstrated
- Evaluated
- Tested

### Retrospective

Team discusses:

- What worked
- What did not work
- What should change
- How to improve future iterations

# Part VIII: Scrum

## 30. Scrum as an Agile Framework

Scrum provides a structured implementation of Agile principles.

Scrum defines:

- Roles
- Events
- Iterations
- Planning practices
- Review practices
- Delivery practices

### Sprint

A Scrum iteration is called a **Sprint**.

Typical length:

- 2–4 weeks

A sprint typically includes:

1. Sprint Planning
2. Development
3. Daily Stand-Ups
4. Testing
5. Sprint Review
6. Delivery
7. Retrospective

A sprint should always produce working, demonstrable features.

---

## 31. Scrum Roles

### Product Owner

Represents customer interests.

Responsibilities:

- Define product vision
- Prioritize features
- Clarify requirements
- Communicate value
- Represent user needs

---

### Scrum Master

Acts primarily as a facilitator.

Responsibilities:

- Support the team
- Facilitate Scrum activities
- Remove obstacles
- Protect team focus
- Promote collaboration

The Scrum Master is **not** a traditional command-and-control manager.

---

### Development Team

Responsible for building the product.

Typical team size:

> 7 ± 2 members (approximately 5–9 people)

#### Problems with Large Teams

- Communication overhead
- Coordination challenges
- Slower decision-making

#### Problems with Small Teams

- Insufficient capacity
- Missing skills
- Lack of expertise diversity

Large projects may use multiple Scrum teams working together.

---

# Part IX: Breaking Down Agile Work

## 32. Product → Features → User Stories → Tasks

### Product

The complete system being developed.

**Example:**

- Online Store Management System

---

### Features

Major capabilities.

Examples:

- Registration/Login
- Product Catalogue
- Product Search
- Product Comparison
- Shopping Cart
- Payment Processing
- Order Tracking

---

### User Stories

Describe functionality from the user perspective.

User stories should explain:

- Who the user is
- What the user wants
- Why it provides value

Examples:

- A customer can create an account.
- A customer can browse products.
- A customer can search for products.
- A customer can compare products.
- A customer can add items to a cart.

---

### Tasks

Technical work required to complete a user story.

Example Shopping Cart Tasks:

- Create database tables
- Build interface
- Program add/remove functions
- Calculate totals
- Test functionality
- Prepare documentation

---

## 33. Feature Breakdown Structure (FBS) vs Work Breakdown Structure (WBS)

### Work Breakdown Structure (WBS)

Traditional approach:

- Project manager decomposes work
- Tasks are assigned to team members
- Work is pushed through the system

### Feature Breakdown Structure (FBS)

Agile approach:

- Organized around product features
- Team members participate in selecting work
- Work is pulled according to priorities and capacity

### Push vs Pull

#### Traditional (Push)

1. Manager creates tasks
2. Manager assigns tasks
3. Team executes tasks

#### Agile (Pull)

1. Team examines priorities
2. Team selects work
3. Team organizes execution

---

## 34. Backlog and Prioritization

Typical backlog categories:

### Current Sprint

Items being developed now.

### Next Sprint

Likely next priorities.

### Future Sprints

Lower-priority future work.

### Process Improvements

Team improvement initiatives.

### Front Burner

Highest-priority work.

### Back Burner

Important but delayed work.

Backlogs are continuously reviewed and reprioritized.

# Part X: Agile Estimation

## 35. Story Points

Story points estimate **relative effort**, not exact time.

They may reflect:

- Complexity
- Amount of work
- Uncertainty
- Risk
- Dependencies

Higher effort or uncertainty results in more story points.

---

## 36. T-Shirt Sizing

Alternative estimation technique:

- Small (S)
- Medium (M)
- Large (L)
- Extra Large (XL)

Example interpretation:

- Small → less than one day
- Medium → approximately one day
- Large → several days
- Extra Large → should be broken down further

Benefits:

- Easier understanding
- Easier estimation
- Easier assignment
- Easier testing
- Easier monitoring

---

## 37. Other Units of Work

Teams may also track:

- Number of tasks
- Number of requirements
- Number of pull requests
- Estimated hours
- Completed features
- Other measurable units

Consistency is more important than the specific unit chosen.

---

# Part XI: Monitoring Agile Progress

## 38. Burndown Charts

Burndown charts show:

> Remaining work over time.

May measure:

- Remaining tasks
- Remaining effort
- Actual progress
- Ideal progress

### Interpretation

#### Above Ideal Line

Team may be behind schedule.

#### Below Ideal Line

Team may be ahead of schedule.

Goal:

- Reach zero remaining work by sprint completion.

---

## 39. Interpreting Burndown Charts

### Early Sprint

Possible issues:

- Confusion
- Communication problems
- Unclear requirements
- Technical obstacles
- Poor estimation

### Mid Sprint

Sharp drops suggest:

- Multiple tasks completed recently

Irregular progress may signal inconsistency.

### Late Sprint

If actual progress converges with the ideal line:

- Coordination has improved
- Obstacles may have been removed
- Delivery is becoming more consistent

### Uses

- Internal monitoring
- Client reporting
- Management reporting
- Schedule risk identification
- Corrective-action decisions

---

## 40. Velocity and Burnup

### Velocity

Measures:

> Amount of work completed during a given time period.

Useful for:

- Sprint planning
- Forecasting
- Team performance assessment

### Burndown

Shows:

> Remaining work.

### Burnup

Shows:

> Completed work growing toward total planned work.

# Part XII: Conditions for Agile Success

## 41. Commitment and Maturity

Agile depends heavily on people.

Successful team members are:

- Responsible
- Motivated
- Collaborative
- Skilled
- Communicative
- Proactive

A motivated Agile team often develops strong ownership of outcomes.

---

## 42. Agile Still Requires Early Definition

Agile does not eliminate planning.

Teams still need:

- Product vision
- Major requirements
- Architecture
- Team structure
- Roles
- Responsibilities
- Priorities
- Expected value

---

## 43. Organizational Support

Agile requires commitment from:

- Company leadership
- Senior management
- Product owner
- Development team
- Other stakeholders

Without support, teams may face:

- Resource shortages
- Delayed decisions
- Conflicting priorities
- External pressures

---

## 44. When Agile Is Most Suitable

Ideal for projects with:

- Evolving requirements
- Frequent releases
- Continuous feedback
- Flexible priorities
- Incremental development

Most commonly associated with software development.

---

## 45. When Agile May Be Less Effective

Less suitable when:

- Work is highly sequential
- Changes are difficult after completion
- Requirements are fixed
- Teams lack maturity
- Customer involvement is limited

Construction is a common example.

---

## 46. Hybrid Project Management

Projects often combine approaches.

Examples:

- Waterfall governance + Agile execution
- Traditional planning + Scrum development
- Overall waterfall structure + Agile subprojects

The appropriate mix depends on:

- Complexity
- Uncertainty
- Constraints
- Stakeholder needs

# Final Takeaways

1. Project management combines formal knowledge with leadership, communication, judgment, and experience.
2. Frameworks provide structure for managing complex projects.
3. PMI is a major organization promoting project management standards and certifications.
4. PMBOK 6 consists of **5 Process Groups**, **10 Knowledge Areas**, and **49 Processes**.
5. Planning is the largest process group (**24 processes**).
6. Integration Management coordinates all project areas and becomes increasingly important as project complexity grows.
7. Time, cost, scope, quality, resources, and risk must be balanced continuously.
8. Waterfall works best when requirements are stable and work is sequential.
9. Construction is a classic example of a waterfall-oriented environment.
10. Agile works best when requirements evolve and value can be delivered incrementally.
11. Scrum implements Agile using sprints, defined roles, reviews, and retrospectives.
12. Scrum's key roles are **Product Owner**, **Scrum Master**, and **Development Team**.
13. Agile work is commonly organized as **Product → Feature → User Story → Task**.
14. Agile estimation methods include Story Points, T-Shirt Sizing, Hours, Tasks, Features, and Pull Requests.
15. Burndown charts track remaining work; Burnup charts track completed work.
16. Agile requires motivated, capable, and collaborative teams.
17. Agile still requires upfront vision, architecture, and role definition.
18. Agile and Waterfall can be combined through hybrid approaches.
19. PMBOK 8 shifts emphasis toward principles and performance domains.
20. No framework is universally best. The appropriate choice depends on project characteristics, uncertainty, stakeholder expectations, team maturity, and required flexibility.

---

> **Core Principle:**  
> There is no universally superior project management framework. Effective project managers select and adapt approaches based on project context, constraints, uncertainty, stakeholder needs, and organizational conditions.
