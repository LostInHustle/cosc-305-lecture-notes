# Lecture Notes: Project Quality Planning, Assurance, and Control

## 1. Lecture Overview

Quality planning is one of the main activities in project planning. A project is not successful simply because it finishes on time and stays within budget. The final product or service must also meet the required level of quality.

This lecture covers:

1. The meaning of quality
2. Requirements and fitness for use
3. Quality planning
4. Quality standards and measurements
5. Statistical quality measures
6. Control limits and normal distributions
7. Sigma levels
8. Quality planning techniques
9. Cost of quality
10. Quality assurance
11. Quality control
12. Continuous process improvement

## 2. The Project Management Triangle

A project is usually evaluated using three major constraints:

1. Time
2. Cost
3. Quality or scope

A project manager must balance all three.

For example, a team may deliver a product on time and within budget, but the project is still unsuccessful if the product does not work properly or does not satisfy the client.

Quality therefore needs to be planned and managed just like time and cost.

## 3. What Is Quality?

Quality describes how well a product or service satisfies the needs for which it was created.

A simple definition is:

> Quality means creating the right product for the stakeholder and making sure it performs its intended function properly.

Several common definitions of quality include:

1. Meeting stakeholder and client needs
2. Building what the team promised to build
3. Conformance to requirements
4. Fitness for use
5. Performing the intended function efficiently and reliably
6. Meeting both stated and implied requirements

A useful summary is:

> Do what you promised, and do it well.

## 4. The Two Main Aspects of Quality

### 4.1 Conformance to Requirements

The product must match the approved requirements and specifications.

For example, if the requirements say that a booking application must allow users to search for rooms and complete reservations, those features must be included and must behave as specified.

Building something different, even if it is technically impressive, does not represent good quality if it does not satisfy the approved requirements.

### 4.2 Fitness for Use

The product must be practical, reliable, safe, and suitable for its intended purpose.

Meeting a written requirement is not always enough. The product must also work properly in real use.

## 5. Nail and Picture Example

Suppose the requirement is to place a nail in a wall so that a picture can be hung.

A person could technically satisfy the requirement by putting a nail in the wall. However, the result may still have poor quality if:

1. The nail is too small
2. The nail cannot support the weight of the picture
3. The nail is placed in a weak part of the wall
4. The nail is not installed correctly
5. The picture falls when someone touches it
6. Normal movement or vibration causes the picture to fall

The requirement has been completed, but the solution is not fit for use.

A high quality solution requires choosing the correct nail, installing it correctly, placing it in a strong location, and making sure it can safely support the picture.

## 6. Stated and Implied Requirements

Quality includes both stated requirements and implied requirements.

### 6.1 Stated Requirements

Stated requirements are formally written in the project documents.

Examples include:

1. The system must allow users to create an account
2. The system must respond within two seconds
3. The table must be 1.5 metres wide
4. The house must contain three bedrooms

### 6.2 Implied Requirements

Implied requirements may not be written explicitly, but they are naturally expected.

For example, a house specification may describe the number of rooms, kitchens, and bathrooms. It may not explicitly state that every room needs a door. However, doors are an obvious and implied requirement.

A team cannot ignore an important feature simply because it was not written explicitly.

### 6.3 Why Implied Requirements Matter

It is impossible to document every tiny expectation. Professional judgment is needed to identify reasonable expectations related to:

1. Safety
2. Usability
3. Reliability
4. Security
5. Accessibility
6. Legal compliance
7. Industry practice

Good quality means satisfying both the formal specification and the reasonable expectations of the client.

## 7. What Is Quality Planning?

Quality planning is the process of deciding:

1. What quality means for the project
2. Which standards must be followed
3. Which characteristics will be measured
4. What values are acceptable
5. How testing will be performed
6. Who will be responsible for each quality activity
7. What evidence will prove that the requirements have been satisfied
8. How quality results will be documented and reported

Quality should be planned before the product is created. Waiting until the end of the project makes quality problems more expensive and difficult to fix.

## 8. Main Steps in Quality Planning

### Step 1: Identify Quality Requirements

Determine what the client, stakeholders, organization, and industry expect from the product.

### Step 2: Select Quality Measures

Decide which measurable characteristics will represent quality.

Examples include:

1. Response time
2. Error rate
3. System availability
4. Number of defects
5. Completion accuracy
6. Customer satisfaction
7. Product dimensions
8. Strength
9. Reliability
10. Security performance

### Step 3: Define Targets and Limits

For every measure, specify:

1. The target value
2. The minimum acceptable value
3. The maximum acceptable value
4. The allowed tolerance
5. The conditions under which the result will be rejected

### Step 4: Design the Tests

Decide how the quality characteristic will be measured.

The plan should identify:

1. The test method
2. The test environment
3. The tools required
4. The sample size
5. The test schedule
6. The person responsible
7. The expected evidence

### Step 5: Assign Responsibilities

Each quality activity needs a clear owner.

The plan should state who will:

1. Conduct the test
2. Review the results
3. Approve the product
4. Report defects
5. Correct problems
6. Confirm that corrections were successful

### Step 6: Document and Report Results

Quality results should be documented so that the team can prove whether the product satisfies the requirements.

## 9. Quality Policy

A quality policy describes an organization’s general commitment and approach to quality.

Many organizations have an existing quality policy that applies to every project.

A project quality plan should align with:

1. The organizational quality policy
2. Business objectives
3. Client requirements
4. Project requirements
5. Industry regulations
6. Professional standards

A quality policy may explain:

1. The organization’s quality principles
2. Required review procedures
3. Required documentation
4. Approval responsibilities
5. Testing expectations
6. Continuous improvement practices

## 10. Standards and Measures of Quality

Quality measures should be connected to a recognized standard whenever a suitable standard exists.

The International Organization for Standardization, commonly called ISO, defines quality as the degree to which a set of inherent characteristics fulfills requirements.

Standards can apply to many products and services, such as:

1. Fuel ratings
2. Hotel ratings
3. Construction materials
4. Steel strength
5. Product dimensions
6. Manufacturing processes
7. Software quality

Standards help organizations create consistent expectations and compare results using an accepted reference.

## 11. Software Quality Characteristics

Software quality can be evaluated through several major characteristics.

A commonly referenced software quality model includes the following areas:

### 11.1 Functional Suitability

Does the software provide the correct functions?

Questions include:

1. Are all required functions available?
2. Are the results correct?
3. Do the features support the user’s goals?

### 11.2 Performance Efficiency

Does the software perform quickly and use resources efficiently?

Possible measures include:

1. Response time
2. Processing time
3. Memory use
4. Processor use
5. Number of supported users

### 11.3 Compatibility

Can the software work with other systems, products, and environments?

Compatibility includes:

1. Data exchange
2. Integration
3. Coexistence with other software
4. Support for required devices and platforms

### 11.4 Usability

Can users understand and operate the software easily?

Usability may include:

1. Ease of learning
2. Ease of navigation
3. Accessibility
4. Error prevention
5. User interface clarity
6. User satisfaction

### 11.5 Reliability

Can the system continue operating correctly over time?

Possible measures include:

1. Failure frequency
2. Recovery time
3. Availability
4. Accuracy
5. Stability under normal use

### 11.6 Security

Does the system protect information and prevent unauthorized actions?

Security may involve:

1. Authentication
2. Authorization
3. Confidentiality
4. Data integrity
5. Accountability
6. Protection against attacks

### 11.7 Maintainability

Can the software be corrected, updated, and improved efficiently?

Maintainability may depend on:

1. Clear code
2. Modular design
3. Documentation
4. Automated testing
5. Ease of identifying defects

### 11.8 Portability

Can the software be moved to another environment?

Examples include moving the software to:

1. Another operating system
2. Another server
3. Another cloud environment
4. Another device type

## 13. Measuring Quality Through Samples

In many situations, it is not practical to test every product or every possible system operation.

Instead, the organization tests a sample.

### 13.1 Manufacturing Example

Suppose a company manufactures tables.

Each table has specifications for:

1. Length
2. Width
3. Height
4. Strength
5. Surface quality

Testing every table might be expensive and slow. The company can select a sample and measure whether the sampled tables satisfy the specifications.

### 13.2 Construction Example

During construction, samples of concrete may be collected and sent to a laboratory.

The laboratory can test properties such as:

1. Compressive strength
2. Durability
3. Consistency
4. Ability to withstand pressure

The results help determine whether the concrete being used is suitable.

### 13.3 Software Example

A software team cannot always test every possible input, device, network condition, and user action.

Instead, the team selects representative tests, such as:

1. Common user activities
2. Important business processes
3. High risk functions
4. Boundary values
5. Failure conditions
6. Different user loads

Sampling saves time and cost, but the sample must be representative. A poorly selected sample can hide serious defects.

## 14. Variation and Tolerance

No production or service process produces exactly the same result every time.

Small variations are normal.

Suppose a table should be 1.5 metres wide. The table may not measure exactly 1.5 metres every time. The organization must decide how much variation is acceptable.

Possible tolerances could include:

1. 1.5 metres plus or minus 1 millimetre
2. 1.5 metres plus or minus 2 millimetres
3. 1.5 metres plus or minus 5 millimetres

A smaller tolerance generally represents stricter quality, but it may also increase the production cost.

The correct tolerance depends on:

1. Customer needs
2. Safety requirements
3. Industry standards
4. Product purpose
5. Production capability
6. Cost
7. Risk

## 15. Control Limits

Control limits define the acceptable range of variation for a process.

There are usually two limits:

1. Upper control limit
2. Lower control limit

Results inside the limits are generally considered acceptable.

Results outside the limits may indicate:

1. A defect
2. An unstable process
3. A measurement problem
4. An equipment issue
5. A change in operating conditions
6. A need for investigation

Management must establish a process that consistently produces results within the selected limits.

## 16. Normal Distribution

Many measurements approximately follow a normal distribution.

A normal distribution has a bell shaped curve.

Most results appear near the centre, while fewer results appear far from the centre.

Important concepts include:

1. Mean
2. Standard deviation
3. Variance
4. Control limits

### 16.1 Mean

The mean is the average value and normally represents the process target.

For example, if a table should be 1.5 metres wide, the mean should be close to 1.5 metres.

### 16.2 Standard Deviation

Standard deviation describes how spread out the measurements are around the mean.

The symbol commonly used for standard deviation is sigma.

A small standard deviation means the results are closely grouped around the mean.

A large standard deviation means the results have greater variation.

### 16.3 Software Response Time Example

Suppose the target response time is 10 milliseconds.

The average may be close to 10 milliseconds, but individual requests can vary because of:

1. Server activity
2. Network traffic
3. Number of active users
4. Database workload
5. Device performance
6. Background processes

The standard deviation helps describe the amount of variation in these response times.

## 17. Sigma Levels

For a normal distribution, sigma levels describe how much of the data is located within a certain distance from the mean.

### 17.1 One Sigma

Approximately 68.3 percent of results fall within one standard deviation of the mean.

This means about 31.7 percent fall outside that range.

### 17.2 Two Sigma

Approximately 95 percent of results fall within two standard deviations of the mean.

About 5 percent fall outside that range.

### 17.3 Three Sigma

Approximately 99.7 percent of results fall within three standard deviations of the mean.

Only about 0.3 percent fall outside that range.

### 17.4 Six Sigma

Six Sigma represents a very high level of process quality.

It aims to produce extremely few defects. In the commonly used Six Sigma model, the often quoted target is approximately 3.4 defects per million opportunities.

### 17.5 Sigma Summary

| Sigma range | Approximate percentage inside the range |
| ----------- | --------------------------------------: |
| One sigma   |                            68.3 percent |
| Two sigma   |                              95 percent |
| Three sigma |                            99.7 percent |
| Six sigma   |          Extremely close to 100 percent |

A higher sigma level usually represents:

1. Less variation
2. Greater consistency
3. Fewer defects
4. Higher quality
5. Greater process control

However, achieving a higher sigma level may require more time, testing, equipment, training, and cost.

## 18. The Relationship Between Tolerance and Quality

Wider limits make it easier for results to remain within the acceptable range.

However, wider limits can also mean that lower quality results are accepted.

Narrower limits require greater precision and usually represent higher quality, but they may increase cost.

The organization must balance:

1. Required quality
2. Customer expectations
3. Safety
4. Risk
5. Process capability
6. Available budget

The goal is not always absolute perfection. The goal is an appropriate level of quality for the product and its intended use.

## 19. Independent Quality Evaluation

The lecture recommends that quality evaluation should be performed by a separate quality group when possible.

The development team may unintentionally overlook its own mistakes. An independent group can provide a more objective evaluation.

A separate quality group can:

1. Review requirements
2. Conduct tests
3. Inspect deliverables
4. Compare results with standards
5. Report defects
6. Confirm that corrections were completed

Independence does not mean that developers have no responsibility for quality. Everyone contributes to quality, but independent evaluation reduces bias.

## 20. Quality Planning Techniques

Several techniques can support quality planning.

### 20.1 Cost Benefit Analysis

Cost benefit analysis compares the expected benefit of a quality activity with its cost.

The highest possible quality is not always economically reasonable.

Questions include:

1. How much will the quality improvement cost?
2. How many defects will it prevent?
3. How much money will it save later?
4. Will it reduce safety or security risks?
5. Will customers notice and value the improvement?
6. Is the improvement worth the investment?

### 20.2 Benchmarking

Benchmarking compares a project or process with:

1. An industry standard
2. A successful competitor
3. A previous project
4. A recognized best practice
5. A high performing internal team

Benchmarking helps establish realistic targets and identify opportunities for improvement.

### 20.3 Design of Experiments

Design of experiments is a structured method for testing how different variables affect a result.

For example, a software team might vary:

1. Number of users
2. Server capacity
3. Database configuration
4. Network speed
5. Request volume

The results can show which factors have the greatest effect on performance.

### 20.4 Cost of Quality Analysis

This technique calculates the money spent to prevent, identify, and correct quality problems.

### 20.5 Control Charts

A control chart displays process results over time.

It normally includes:

1. A centre line
2. An upper control limit
3. A lower control limit
4. Measurements collected over time

A control chart helps identify unusual variation or signs that the process is becoming unstable.

### 20.6 Cause and Effect Diagram

A cause and effect diagram helps identify possible causes of a quality problem.

Common cause categories include:

1. People
2. Methods
3. Machines
4. Materials
5. Measurements
6. Environment

It is also commonly called a fishbone diagram or Ishikawa diagram.

### 20.7 Check Sheet

A check sheet is a structured form used to collect and count occurrences.

It can track:

1. Defect types
2. Failure locations
3. Complaint categories
4. Error frequency
5. Inspection results

### 20.8 Histogram

A histogram displays how frequently values occur within different ranges.

It helps the team understand:

1. The centre of the data
2. The spread of the data
3. Variation
4. Unusual patterns
5. Possible outliers

### 20.9 Pareto Chart

A Pareto chart organizes problems from the most frequent or costly to the least frequent or costly.

It is connected to the idea that a small number of causes may create a large proportion of the problems.

The team should usually focus first on the causes with the greatest effect.

## 21. Cost of Quality

The cost of quality includes all costs related to achieving quality and dealing with quality problems.

The four major categories are:

1. Prevention costs
2. Appraisal costs
3. Internal failure costs
4. External failure costs

The total cost of quality can be expressed as:

Total cost of quality equals prevention costs plus appraisal costs plus internal failure costs plus external failure costs.

## 22. Prevention Costs

Prevention costs are spent to stop defects from occurring.

Examples include:

1. Better system design
2. Employee training
3. Clear requirements
4. Process improvement
5. Design reviews
6. Reliable tools
7. Database redundancy
8. Automated validation
9. Security planning
10. Coding standards

### Booking Application Example

Suppose a booking application uses only one database. If that database fails, the entire application may stop working.

The team could use:

1. A backup database
2. Database replication
3. Load balancing
4. Automatic recovery
5. Better monitoring

These solutions increase the initial cost, but they can prevent future system failures.

## 23. Appraisal Costs

Appraisal costs come from evaluating whether the product satisfies quality requirements.

Examples include:

1. Inspections
2. Testing
3. Quality reviews
4. Performance testing
5. Security testing
6. Audits
7. Test equipment
8. Test environment maintenance

For a booking application, appraisal may include testing whether the system can support the expected number of users without slowing down or crashing.

## 24. Internal Failure Costs

Internal failure costs occur when a defect is discovered before the product is delivered.

Examples include:

1. Correcting bugs found during testing
2. Rewriting defective code
3. Repeating failed tests
4. Reworking a design
5. Replacing defective materials
6. Delays caused by corrections

Finding defects internally is still costly, but it is usually less expensive than allowing customers to discover them.

## 25. External Failure Costs

External failure costs occur after the product has been delivered or released.

Examples include:

1. Customer support
2. Emergency corrections
3. Refunds
4. Warranty claims
5. Service outages
6. Lost customers
7. Legal consequences
8. Damage to the organization’s reputation
9. Lost business opportunities
10. Security incident response

External failures are often the most expensive because they affect both the product and the customer’s trust.

## 26. Cost of Quality Example

Consider the following example:

| Cost category    | Current process | Improved process |
| ---------------- | --------------: | ---------------: |
| Prevention       |     400 dollars |      700 dollars |
| Appraisal        |     600 dollars |      600 dollars |
| Internal failure |     800 dollars |      600 dollars |
| External failure |    2200 dollars |      900 dollars |
| Total            |    4000 dollars |     2800 dollars |

The improved process spends 300 dollars more on prevention.

However:

1. Internal failure cost decreases by 200 dollars
2. External failure cost decreases by 1300 dollars
3. Total cost decreases by 1200 dollars

This shows that spending more on prevention can reduce the total cost of quality.

## 27. Why Prevention Can Reduce Total Cost

When quality is designed into the product from the beginning:

1. Fewer defects are created
2. Less rework is needed
3. Testing finds fewer serious problems
4. Customers experience fewer failures
5. Support costs decrease
6. Reputation is protected
7. The total cost may decrease

A cheap initial design may appear to save money, but the later cost of correcting defects can be much greater.

## 28. Quality Versus Perfection

Quality planning does not mean investing unlimited time and money to achieve perfection.

There is a point where additional improvement may cost more than the benefit it creates.

The project team must decide:

1. Is the current quality level safe?
2. Does it meet client expectations?
3. Does it satisfy legal and professional standards?
4. What is the risk of leaving the remaining defects?
5. How expensive would later correction be?
6. Is further improvement worth the cost?
7. Will delaying delivery create a larger problem?

Some minor issues may reasonably be corrected later if:

1. They do not affect safety
2. They do not affect essential functions
3. They do not create serious security risks
4. Their effect on users is small
5. The cost of immediate correction is unusually high
6. There is a clear plan for future correction

The team should not use this reasoning to ignore serious defects.

## 29. Continuous Improvement

Quality planning does not end when the project is delivered.

Teams should review completed work and improve their processes.

A reflection or lessons learned meeting can examine:

1. What worked well
2. What caused defects
3. Which tests were useful
4. Which problems were discovered too late
5. Whether the quality targets were realistic
6. Whether responsibilities were clear
7. Which tools should be improved
8. How similar problems can be prevented

The goal is to improve both the product and the process used to create it.

## 30. Quality Assurance

Quality assurance focuses on the processes used to create a quality product.

It asks:

> Are we following appropriate processes that should lead to good quality?

Quality assurance activities may include:

1. Establishing development procedures
2. Creating coding standards
3. Training team members
4. Conducting process audits
5. Reviewing project methods
6. Improving documentation
7. Standardizing testing procedures
8. Preventing defects
9. Reviewing whether the quality plan is being followed

Quality assurance is mainly process focused and preventive.

## 31. Quality Control

Quality control focuses on examining the actual product or service.

It asks:

> Does the completed result satisfy the required quality level?

Quality control activities may include:

1. Product inspection
2. Software testing
3. Measuring dimensions
4. Comparing results with control limits
5. Identifying defects
6. Rejecting unacceptable outputs
7. Confirming that corrections work

Quality control is mainly product focused and detection focused.

## 32. Quality Assurance Compared With Quality Control

| Area           | Quality assurance                       | Quality control                             |
| -------------- | --------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| Main focus     | Process                                 | Product or result                           |
| Main goal      | Prevent defects                         | Find defects                                |
| Main question  | Are we using the correct process?       | Does the result meet the requirement?       |
| Timing         | Throughout planning and production      | During testing and inspection               |
| Examples       | Procedures, training, audits, standards | Testing, inspection, measurement            |
| Orientation    | Preventive                              | Detective and corrective                    |
| Responsibility | Usually shared across the organization  | Often performed by testing or quality teams |

A simple way to remember the distinction is:

> Quality assurance improves how the work is done. Quality control checks whether the result is acceptable.

## 33. Worked Software Example

Suppose a booking application has the following requirement:

> At least 95 percent of booking requests must complete within two seconds.

### Quality Planning

The team defines:

1. The response time target
2. The required 95 percent success level
3. The expected number of simultaneous users
4. The test environment
5. The test sample size
6. The responsible testing team
7. The evidence required for approval

### Quality Assurance

The team improves the development process by:

1. Creating performance standards
2. Reviewing database design
3. Training developers
4. Using code reviews
5. Adding automated performance tests
6. Monitoring whether the approved process is followed

### Quality Control

The team runs a performance test and measures:

1. Number of requests
2. Response time for every request
3. Percentage completed within two seconds
4. Failed requests
5. Average response time
6. Variation in response time

If only 90 percent of requests finish within two seconds, the system does not satisfy the requirement.

## 34. Common Quality Planning Mistakes

### 34.1 Using Vague Targets

A statement such as “the system should be fast” is difficult to test.

A stronger requirement is:

> At least 95 percent of requests must complete within two seconds under the expected user load.

### 34.2 Measuring the Wrong Characteristic

A team may focus on speed while ignoring security, reliability, or accuracy.

The selected measures must represent what matters to the user.

### 34.3 Ignoring Implied Requirements

A product may match the written specification but still be unsafe or unusable.

### 34.4 Testing Too Late

Defects become more expensive to correct as the project progresses.

### 34.5 Using an Unrepresentative Sample

A small or biased sample may make a poor quality process appear acceptable.

### 34.6 Confusing Control Limits With Requirements

Control limits describe the behaviour of the process. Specification limits describe what the customer or design requires.

A process can be stable but still produce unacceptable results if it is consistently centred on the wrong value.

### 34.7 Assuming More Testing Creates Quality

Testing can reveal defects, but it does not automatically create a good product. Quality should be built into the requirements, design, process, and implementation.

### 34.8 Pursuing Perfection Without Considering Cost

Extremely strict quality targets may not provide enough benefit to justify their cost.

## 35. Key Formulas and Figures

### Total Cost of Quality

Total cost of quality equals:

Prevention costs plus appraisal costs plus internal failure costs plus external failure costs

### Common Sigma Percentages

1. One sigma contains approximately 68.3 percent of results
2. Two sigma contains approximately 95 percent of results
3. Three sigma contains approximately 99.7 percent of results
4. Six Sigma is commonly associated with approximately 3.4 defects per million opportunities

1. What does quality mean for this project?
2. What are the stated requirements?
3. What reasonable implied requirements exist?
4. Which standards apply?
5. Which quality characteristics will be measured?
6. What is the target for each measure?
7. What tolerance is acceptable?
8. What are the upper and lower limits?
9. How will each characteristic be tested?
10. What sample will be used?
11. Who is responsible for testing?
12. What evidence is required?
13. Who approves the result?

15. How will defects be reported?
16. How will improvements be documented?

## 37. Main Takeaways

1. A successful project must manage time, cost, and quality.
2. Quality means satisfying requirements and being fit for use.
3. Both stated and implied requirements matter.
4. Quality must be planned before development begins.
5. Every quality target should be measurable.
6. Tolerances and control limits define acceptable variation.
7. Sampling is often necessary because testing everything may be impractical.
8. Standard deviation and sigma levels help describe variation.
9. Higher quality usually reduces defects but may increase initial cost.
10. Prevention can reduce internal and external failure costs.
11. External failures are often more expensive than internal failures.
12. Quality assurance focuses on improving and following processes.
13. Quality control focuses on testing and evaluating the result.
14. Independent quality evaluation can reduce bias.
15. The best quality level balances client needs, risk, cost, and benefit.
16. Teams should continuously improve their quality processes.

## 38. Short Exam Review

### What is quality?

Quality is the degree to which a product or service satisfies stated and implied requirements and is fit for its intended use.

### What is quality planning?

Quality planning identifies quality requirements, measures, targets, tolerances, testing methods, responsibilities, and evidence.

### What is fitness for use?

Fitness for use means the product can safely and effectively perform its intended purpose.

### What are control limits?

Control limits define the range within which process variation is considered acceptable or stable.

### What is sigma?

Sigma represents standard deviation, which measures how spread out results are around the mean.

### What is the cost of quality?

The cost of quality is the total of prevention, appraisal, internal failure, and external failure costs.

### What is quality assurance?

Quality assurance uses processes, standards, reviews, and audits to prevent quality problems.

### What is quality control?

Quality control tests and inspects actual outputs to determine whether they are acceptable.

### Why are external failures expensive?

They may require corrections after release and can also cause customer dissatisfaction, lost business, legal consequences, and reputational damage.
