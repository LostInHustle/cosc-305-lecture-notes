# Creating and Operating a Program Management Office

## Introduction

This lecture examines how a Program Management Office, commonly called a PMO, can support a large and complex organizational transformation.

The case discussed in the lecture involved the implementation of Workday Student at the University of British Columbia. This initiative was known as the Integrated Renewal Program Student, or IRP Student. It involved hundreds of team members, many project managers, multiple leadership groups, and numerous related projects.

The initiative was described as a program rather than a single project because it contained many connected projects that collectively contributed to one major outcome. That outcome was the transition from the university's previous student information system to Workday Student.

The central purpose of the PMO was to create structure, coordination, visibility, and transparency across the program. It helped project teams organize and report their work, while also giving senior leaders a reliable view of overall progress, risks, issues, and changes.

# 1. Acknowledging the Institutional Context

The lecture began with a land acknowledgement recognizing that the two main campuses of the University of British Columbia are located on the traditional, ancestral, and unceded territories of Indigenous peoples.

A land acknowledgement recognizes the historical and continuing relationships between Indigenous peoples and their territories. Within an institutional setting, it can also encourage participants to consider how organizational activities occur within broader social, cultural, and historical contexts.

# 2. Understanding the Program

## 2.1 Why This Was a Program

A project is normally a temporary effort intended to create a particular product, service, or result.

A program is a collection of related projects and activities that are coordinated to achieve a larger strategic outcome.

IRP Student was considered a program because it included numerous projects related to areas such as:

1. System development
2. System configuration
3. Testing
4. Technical delivery
5. Student services
6. Organizational change
7. Stakeholder engagement
8. Finance
9. Governance
10. Program administration

Each project contributed to the implementation of Workday Student, but each one had its own scope, activities, schedule, resources, and dependencies.

Managing these projects independently would not have been sufficient. The projects had to be coordinated because the output of one team often became the input of another team.

## 2.2 The Air Traffic Analogy

The lecturer compared the program to an airport with many aircraft in the air.

The aircraft represented different projects. Some projects were large, like jumbo jets. Others were smaller, like helicopters or light aircraft. Each project had a different purpose, size, schedule, and destination.

Some aircraft were taking off while others were landing. They were using different runways at different times. Without coordination, aircraft could experience delays, conflicts, or dangerous situations.

In the same way, a large program requires coordination so that projects:

1. Move in the same strategic direction
2. Complete work in the correct sequence
3. Provide outputs to other teams at the required time
4. Avoid conflicting plans
5. Identify delays early
6. Use organizational resources effectively

The PMO performed a role similar to air traffic control. It did not personally fly every aircraft. Instead, it created coordination, visibility, standards, and early warning mechanisms.

## 2.3 The Scale of the Program

At its peak, the program involved more than 400 team members and approximately 50 project managers.

At different stages, there could be 50 or more projects active at the same time. These projects did not all follow the same method because they involved different types of work.

For example, a technical development project could require design, development, testing, and deployment. A change management project could involve communication, training, consultation, and stakeholder readiness.

This variety created a significant management challenge. Senior leaders needed one understandable view of the program even though the teams were using different methods and performing different types of work.

# 3. Why a Program Management Office Was Needed

## 3.1 Managing Complexity

The PMO was created because the program was too large and interconnected to be managed through informal communication alone.

Without a coordinating function, teams might use different definitions, reporting standards, planning methods, and decision processes. This could make it difficult to determine whether the entire program was progressing as expected.

The PMO helped manage this complexity by establishing common frameworks for:

1. Planning
2. Status reporting
3. Risk management
4. Issue management
5. Change management
6. Decision tracking
7. Financial monitoring
8. Milestone reviews
9. Governance reporting

Standardization did not mean that every team had to perform its work in exactly the same way. Teams could retain methods appropriate to their work. However, selected information had to be recorded and reported consistently so that it could be combined at the program level.

## 3.2 The Main Questions

One of the first questions asked by the PMO was:

What questions does governance want answered?

This question helped determine the purpose of reporting. Reporting should not exist simply because an organization has always produced reports. It should provide information that supports oversight, decisions, and action.

Governance wanted answers to questions such as:

1. Is the program on track?
2. Are planned deliverables being completed?
3. Are sufficient resources available?
4. What risks could affect the program?
5. What issues have already occurred?
6. Which activities are falling behind schedule?
7. What corrective actions are being taken?
8. Are significant changes being controlled?
9. Are project dependencies being managed?
10. What support is required from senior leadership?

These questions shaped the PMO frameworks and reporting processes.

# 4. The PMO Mandate

The PMO supported both delivery teams and senior governance.

For delivery teams, it provided standards, tools, guidance, and reporting frameworks.

For governance, it created an integrated and understandable view of the program.

The PMO concentrated on four major areas of project and program management.

## 4.1 Integration

Integration involved connecting information from many teams and projects.

Each project manager could understand the work within a particular project, but senior leaders needed to understand how all projects worked together. The PMO created this broader perspective.

Integration was especially important when one team's schedule or output affected another team's work.

## 4.2 Schedule

Schedule management helped determine whether activities and deliverables were being completed on time.

The PMO did not create every detailed task in every project plan. Project managers were responsible for planning their own work. However, the PMO established standards for selecting and reporting major activities and milestones.

## 4.3 Risk

Risk management involved identifying uncertain events or conditions that could affect program objectives.

The PMO created a common risk framework so that risks could be consistently described, assessed, monitored, and reported.

## 4.4 Stakeholders and Governance

The PMO acted as an important communication connection between delivery teams and governance.

It consolidated information from project managers and presented program status to the steering committee and other senior leaders.

# 5. The Consolidated Program Plan

## 5.1 Purpose of the Consolidated Plan

One of the PMO's most important products was the program level consolidated plan.

Every project manager maintained one or more detailed plans. These plans could contain hundreds or thousands of tasks.

Governance could not reasonably review every task in every plan. The PMO therefore selected important elements from the detailed plans and combined them into a higher level consolidated view.

The consolidated plan provided an integrated picture of:

1. Major deliverables
2. Important dates
3. Key dependencies
4. Program milestones
5. Progress against expectations
6. Activities that were at risk of delay
7. Activities that were already delayed

The consolidated plan was not intended to replace detailed project plans. It served a different purpose. Detailed plans supported day to day project management, while the consolidated plan supported program coordination and governance.

## 5.2 Developing the Plans

Planning began with high level workshops.

During these workshops, participants discussed:

1. What work was expected
2. When the work was expected
3. Major activities
4. Important dependencies
5. Constraints
6. Upstream requirements
7. Downstream impacts

The term upstream refers to work or information that must be completed before another activity can begin.

The term downstream refers to teams, activities, or outcomes that depend on the current work.

After the workshops, individual teams developed detailed plans. The teams then reviewed the plans together to check alignment.

Once the detailed plans were sufficiently developed, selected elements were incorporated into the consolidated program plan.

## 5.3 The Scale of Planning

The consolidated plan eventually contained approximately 4,000 to 5,000 tracked items.

Some individual functional plans were even larger. For example, the technology delivery plan contained more than 8,000 items.

These figures demonstrate why program planning was an ongoing activity rather than a one time event.

Plans evolved because:

1. New information became available
2. Dependencies became clearer
3. Requirements changed
4. Risks affected planned activities
5. Delivery dates changed
6. Projects entered new phases
7. Additional work was identified

A useful lesson is that a plan should be controlled, but it should not be treated as permanently fixed. A good plan is updated through an authorized and visible process.

# 6. Beginning With the End in Mind

The phrase beginning with the end in mind was an important theme throughout the lecture.

It means that a team should understand the desired outcome and reporting requirements before designing its processes.

For example, if the program must eventually explain the difference between the original budget and the final cost, it should track approved financial changes throughout the program.

If the program waits until closure to reconstruct that information, important records may be missing.

This principle can be applied by asking questions such as:

1. What must be demonstrated at program closure?
2. What evidence will be required?
3. What information should be collected during delivery?
4. Who is responsible for maintaining that information?
5. How will changes be documented?
6. How will completion be formally approved?

Planning for closure at the beginning makes the final review more accurate and efficient.

# 7. Visibility and Transparency

The senior leadership guidance was clear:

Governance wanted full visibility and transparency regarding progress, risks, and issues. Governance did not want surprises.

This principle shaped the culture of reporting.

A status report should not attempt to make a project appear healthier than it is. Its purpose is to present an accurate picture so that action can be taken when necessary.

A useful reporting culture encourages people to communicate:

1. What has been completed
2. What remains incomplete
3. What is beginning to move off track
4. What has already become overdue
5. What support is needed
6. What corrective action is planned

Transparency is not the same as blame. In an effective program, reporting a problem early is considered responsible behaviour.

# 8. Reporting Relationships

Project managers had a direct reporting relationship with the senior leader responsible for their functional area.

This senior leader helped with:

1. Work assignments
2. Resource decisions
3. Barrier removal
4. Functional direction
5. Human resource matters
6. Delivery priorities

Project managers also had a secondary reporting responsibility to the PMO for program planning and reporting standards.

This secondary relationship allowed the PMO to request consistent updates and hold project managers accountable for program level reporting.

The PMO did not replace functional leadership. Instead, it created coordination across functional boundaries.

# 9. Program Status Reporting

## 9.1 Purpose of the Status Report

The status report gave governance a summarized view of program health.

The first page usually presented the most important information, including:

1. Overall program health
2. Scope status
3. Resource status
4. Schedule status
5. Recent accomplishments
6. Upcoming activities
7. Areas requiring attention
8. Major risks
9. Major issues
10. Support required from governance

More detailed information appeared later in the report.

The content of the report evolved as the program moved through different phases. This is important because governance information needs may change over time.

## 9.2 Weekly Reporting

The program followed a weekly reporting cycle.

The process generally involved:

1. Teams submitted updates by Tuesday
2. The PMO consolidated the updates by Wednesday
3. Program delivery leadership completed a preliminary review on Thursday
4. The report was prepared and published
5. The steering committee reviewed the report the following Tuesday

This created a regular rhythm for collecting, reviewing, and discussing information.

A consistent reporting rhythm helps teams know:

1. When updates are required
2. What information must be provided
3. When concerns will be reviewed
4. When governance decisions may occur
5. Who is responsible at each stage

# 10. Status Indicators and Early Warning

## 10.1 Why Amber Status Matters

The lecturer emphasized the importance of amber status.

Amber usually means that an activity is not yet overdue, but it is beginning to move away from the approved plan.

Teams may be tempted to report green until a deadline has actually been missed. This approach reduces the time available for corrective action.

Amber should be treated as an early warning.

An amber status can indicate that:

1. Progress is slower than expected
2. A dependency may not be available on time
3. A resource shortage is affecting delivery
4. Quality problems require additional work
5. An approval is taking longer than expected
6. A risk is becoming more likely
7. The remaining schedule has little flexibility

The PMO monitored amber items and looked for patterns. If an item remained amber for several reporting periods, the PMO could request a deeper review.

## 10.2 Amber Status, Risks, and Issues

These concepts are related but different.

An amber status indicates that work is trending away from the plan.

A risk is an uncertain event or condition that may affect objectives.

An issue is a problem that has already occurred or is certain to occur.

For example:

A required resource might become unavailable next month. This is a risk.

A required resource has already left the project. This is an issue.

A task is still within its deadline, but progress suggests it may be late. This is an amber status.

Distinguishing among these concepts supports better decisions.

# 11. The Role of the Steering Committee

The steering committee played a critical role in program success.

An effective steering committee should not simply ask the delivery team to solve every problem alone. It should use its authority and influence to remove barriers.

A constructive steering committee asks:

1. What support do you need?
2. Which decision is preventing progress?
3. Do priorities need to be clarified?
4. Is additional authority required?
5. Does a conflict between areas need senior intervention?
6. Should resources be reassigned?

The program benefited from a steering committee that responded to concerns by asking how it could help.

This behaviour encouraged transparency because project teams knew that reporting a problem could lead to useful action rather than automatic blame.

# 12. Communication and the Community of Practice

The PMO maintained close communication with project managers because they supplied much of the information used in program reporting.

One important communication mechanism was the project management community of practice.

A community of practice is a group of people who share knowledge and improve their professional practices through regular interaction.

The community of practice was used to:

1. Explain reporting standards
2. Introduce risk and issue frameworks
3. Discuss planning methods
4. Provide education
5. Share lessons
6. Receive feedback
7. Identify process problems
8. Simplify reporting requirements

The sessions were not only for delivering instructions. Project managers could explain when a process was too time consuming or did not meet practical needs.

The PMO could then modify and streamline the process.

This demonstrates an important principle. Standardization should create value. A process should be adjusted if the effort required to follow it is greater than the value it produces.

# 13. Supporting Management Frameworks

The PMO created several supporting frameworks.

These included:

1. Milestone management
2. Risk management
3. Issue management
4. Change management
5. Decision management

The same framework could often be used at both the project and program levels.

A project manager could use the risk framework to manage a risk that affected only one project. If the risk became significant enough to affect the wider program or require governance support, it could be elevated into program reporting.

This approach provided consistency while allowing matters to be managed at the appropriate level.

# 14. Milestone Reviews

## 14.1 Meaning of a Milestone

A milestone is a significant point in a project or program that is used to evaluate progress.

A milestone does not necessarily mean that all work is complete. It may represent the completion of an important portion of work.

For example, a milestone might require 80 percent of design activities to be completed by a particular date.

The remaining design work may continue after the milestone, but reaching the expected percentage shows whether the program is progressing at the required pace.

## 14.2 Purpose of Milestone Reviews

Milestone reviews allowed the program to pause and formally examine progress over a period of several months.

The review asked:

1. What did the program intend to achieve?
2. What was actually achieved?
3. Which deliverables remain incomplete?
4. What challenges affected delivery?
5. What risks require attention?
6. What lessons were learned?
7. Do any processes need to change?
8. Is the program prepared for the next stage?

Milestone reviews also created opportunities to recognize achievements and reflect on difficulties.

The first program used approximately 17 milestone reviews. A later program had completed approximately seven milestone reviews at the time of the lecture.

# 15. Risk Management

## 15.1 Definition of Risk

A risk is an uncertain event or condition that may have a positive or negative effect on project or program objectives.

In practice, risk reporting often focuses on potential threats to scope, schedule, cost, quality, resources, or stakeholder outcomes.

The PMO helped teams apply a common risk process.

The process included:

1. Identifying the risk
2. Analyzing the risk
3. Planning a response
4. Implementing the response
5. Monitoring the risk
6. Reporting significant changes

## 15.2 Inherent and Residual Risk

Inherent risk is the level of risk before planned responses or controls are applied.

Residual risk is the level of risk remaining after responses or controls have been implemented.

For example, suppose a key technical specialist is the only person who understands a critical component.

Before any response, the risk may be high because the specialist's absence could stop progress.

The team may respond by documenting the work, training another team member, and creating backup support.

After these actions, the risk may remain, but its likelihood or impact may be lower. This remaining exposure is the residual risk.

Reporting both inherent and residual risk helps governance understand the original exposure and the effectiveness of the mitigation actions.

## 15.3 Ownership of Risks

The PMO did not identify and own every risk.

Project managers and functional teams were responsible for identifying risks within their areas. The PMO supported them by providing definitions, templates, assessment methods, reporting standards, and guidance.

This distinction is important. The PMO supports risk management, but delivery teams remain responsible for managing risks associated with their work.

# 16. Issue Management

An issue is a problem that has already happened or is certain to happen.

Issue management generally includes:

1. Recording the issue
2. Assessing its impact
3. Assigning an owner
4. Developing corrective action
5. Setting a target resolution date
6. Monitoring progress
7. Escalating when required
8. Closing the issue when resolved

One of the PMO's continuing responsibilities was to help teams distinguish between risks and issues.

If an event had not occurred and might not occur, it was generally a risk.

If it had already occurred, it was an issue.

Using consistent definitions prevented confusion and supported accurate reporting.

# 17. Change Management and Change Control

## 17.1 Why Formal Change Control Was Needed

In a large program, a change to one project can affect many other projects.

A date change may alter testing, training, communication, deployment, resource, or stakeholder plans.

Even when project managers communicate directly, a program level change process helps ensure that changes are visible across the program.

The PMO created processes for both formal change requests and change notifications.

A formal change request was appropriate for a significant change requiring analysis and approval.

A change notification could be appropriate for a smaller change that needed to be communicated but did not require the same level of approval.

## 17.2 Impact Analysis Requires Resources

A particularly important lesson involved the cost of assessing a proposed change.

A person might request additional scope, such as 14 new reports. Before the team can approve or reject the request, it must analyze the potential impact.

This analysis may require team members to stop their planned work.

The team should therefore estimate:

1. Who must participate in the analysis
2. How long the analysis will take
3. Which planned tasks will be delayed
4. What technical investigation is required
5. Whether other teams must be consulted
6. Whether funding is needed

Approval may be required before the impact analysis begins.

This creates an important decision for the requester:

Should the team interrupt current work to assess the proposed change?

In some cases, the requester may withdraw or postpone the request after understanding the effort required for analysis.

## 17.3 Preventing Scope Creep

Scope creep occurs when project scope gradually expands without sufficient analysis, approval, funding, or schedule adjustment.

It often happens through many small requests rather than one major decision.

To reduce scope creep, a project manager should ask:

1. Is the request within the approved scope?
2. What value will the change provide?
3. What is the effect on schedule?
4. What is the effect on cost?
5. What resources are required?
6. What existing work will be delayed?
7. Are new risks created?
8. Who has the authority to approve the change?

A project manager is justified in asking whether the priority is to continue planned work or pause to assess a new request.

# 18. Process Iteration and Improvement

The PMO frameworks were not created perfectly in one attempt.

For example, the consolidated plan went through several iterations, and the change management process was revised multiple times.

The process of improvement involved:

1. Creating an initial approach
2. Applying it in practice
3. Gathering feedback
4. Identifying unnecessary effort
5. Adding missing controls
6. Simplifying complicated steps
7. Repeating the cycle

This approach recognizes that a PMO must learn from actual program needs.

A process can initially require more structure because the organization is moving from informal practices to organized practices. Later, the PMO may discover opportunities to streamline the process without losing necessary control.

# 19. Program Closure

## 19.1 Purpose of Closure

Program closure formally determines whether the program has completed its intended work and can be closed.

Closure helps the organization:

1. Confirm completion of deliverables
2. Obtain formal sign off
3. Complete handovers
4. Close financial records
5. Release resources
6. Document remaining gaps
7. Record lessons learned
8. Produce a final report
9. Preserve useful information for future projects

Closure is more than an administrative formality. It demonstrates accountability and creates organizational knowledge.

## 19.2 Closed Versus Able to Be Closed

A useful distinction was made between a program that is closed and one that can be closed.

Some programs have a fixed closing date. When that date arrives, resources are released and funding ends.

Other projects may continue if the sponsor determines that additional work is required.

The closure process therefore provides evidence that helps governance decide whether:

1. The planned work has been completed
2. Remaining work is acceptable
3. Outstanding items can be transferred to operations
4. The project should be extended
5. Additional funding is justified
6. Formal closure can proceed

## 19.3 Main Closure Activities

Major closure activities include:

1. Demonstrating completion of planned deliverables
2. Obtaining acceptance from authorized stakeholders
3. Handing over products and services to operational teams
4. Completing contracts and financial records
5. Closing risks, issues, and changes where possible
6. Recording items that will remain open after closure
7. Conducting lessons learned sessions
8. Preparing the closure report
9. Releasing team members and other resources

## 19.4 Risks During Closure

Closure introduces several risks.

Team members may become interested in their next assignments and reduce attention to the current program.

Resources may be removed before documentation or handover is complete.

Formal sign off may be delayed because outstanding items remain unresolved.

Important lessons may be lost if participants leave before the closure review.

These risks can be reduced by planning closure early, assigning clear responsibilities, and explaining that closure activities are part of the program's required work.

# 20. Lessons Learned

Lessons learned should record both successes and challenges.

Useful questions include:

1. What worked well?
2. What did not work well?
3. What should be repeated?
4. What should be avoided?
5. Which assumptions were incorrect?
6. Which risks occurred?
7. Which responses were effective?
8. Which processes created unnecessary effort?
9. Which decisions should have been made earlier?
10. What should future project teams know?

Future project teams should review relevant lessons learned during initiation and planning.

Lessons learned have limited value if they are recorded but never used.

# 21. Different Types of PMOs

There is no single PMO structure that is appropriate for every organization.

A PMO may focus primarily on support, control, reporting, delivery, or a combination of these responsibilities.

## 21.1 Supporting PMO

A supporting PMO provides templates, guidance, training, tools, and advice.

Project managers retain a high degree of independence.

## 21.2 Reporting PMO

A reporting PMO consolidates project information and prepares reports for senior leaders.

Its main focus is visibility and transparency.

## 21.3 Controlling PMO

A controlling PMO establishes required standards and checks whether teams follow them.

It may define planning, reporting, risk, issue, and change processes.

## 21.4 Delivery PMO

A delivery PMO may directly manage project managers, business analysts, and delivery teams.

It is responsible not only for reporting but also for producing project outcomes.

## 21.5 Choosing a PMO Model

The appropriate PMO model depends on:

1. Organizational needs
2. Program size
3. Program complexity
4. Governance expectations
5. Available resources
6. Existing project management capability
7. Regulatory or accountability requirements
8. The level of authority assigned to the PMO

The PMO should be designed to solve real organizational problems rather than copying another organization's structure.

# 22. PMO Maturity

PMOs can operate at different levels of maturity.

## 22.1 Ad Hoc

At the ad hoc level, practices are informal and inconsistent.

Success may depend heavily on individual experience.

## 22.2 Structured

At the structured level, common processes and responsibilities begin to appear.

Teams have clearer expectations regarding planning and reporting.

## 22.3 Standardized

At the standardized level, common frameworks are consistently used across many projects.

The organization can more easily compare and combine project information.

## 22.4 Managed

At the managed level, performance is measured and processes are actively monitored.

Leaders can use reliable data to make decisions and improve delivery.

## 22.5 Optimized

At the optimized level, the organization continuously evaluates and improves its project and program management practices.

Higher maturity is not automatically better in every situation. Greater maturity requires systems, training, time, governance effort, and administrative work.

The goal is to select the level of maturity that meets organizational needs without creating unnecessary complexity.

# 23. Major Lessons for Students

## 23.1 Large Programs Require Integration

Individual project success does not guarantee program success.

Projects must be coordinated so that dependencies, schedules, resources, and outcomes remain aligned.

## 23.2 Accurate Reporting Supports Action

Status reporting should represent reality rather than create the appearance of success.

Early reporting gives leaders time to respond.

## 23.3 Amber Is a Valuable Warning

Teams should not wait until a deadline is missed before reporting concern.

Amber status creates an opportunity for corrective action.

## 23.4 Governance Must Provide Support

Governance should remove barriers, make decisions, and provide strategic direction.

It should not simply receive reports.

## 23.5 Standardization Should Be Practical

Standards help combine information across teams, but excessive process can slow delivery.

The PMO should continually examine whether its processes provide sufficient value.

## 23.6 Change Requires Analysis

A change request is not free to assess.

Impact analysis consumes time and resources and may affect planned work.

## 23.7 Closure Should Be Planned Early

Teams should know from the beginning what evidence will be required at closure.

This allows necessary information to be collected throughout the program.

## 23.8 PMOs Must Fit Their Context

There is no universal PMO structure.

The PMO's responsibilities and authority should reflect the needs of the organization and the program.

# 24. Practical Example

Consider a university implementing a new course registration system.

The technical team expects to complete system configuration by June.

The testing team expects to begin testing in July.

The training team expects to develop training materials using the final system configuration.

In May, the technical team reports that configuration is progressing more slowly than expected. The June deadline has not yet been missed, but the remaining work is substantial.

This activity should be reported as amber.

The PMO reviews the situation and identifies several possible effects:

1. Testing may begin late
2. Training materials may be delayed
3. User acceptance testing may have less time
4. Deployment could be affected
5. Additional technical resources may be required

The PMO helps bring the affected teams together. The technical team develops a recovery plan, governance approves temporary resource support, and the training team begins work using validated draft materials.

This example demonstrates how visibility and early action can prevent an amber condition from becoming a major issue.

# Conclusion

A Program Management Office can provide essential coordination within a large transformation program. Its value comes from creating an integrated view, supporting consistent practices, enabling transparent reporting, identifying early warning signs, and connecting delivery teams with governance.

The PMO in this case did not perform every project activity. Instead, it created the structures through which many teams could plan, communicate, report, and make decisions consistently.

The most important principle was straightforward: governance needed full visibility into progress, risks, and issues, with no unexpected surprises.

Achieving that level of transparency required reliable plans, common definitions, regular reporting, supportive governance, disciplined change management, and continuous process improvement.
